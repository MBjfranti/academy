# How to write one of these

`article-pattern.md` holds the measured shape: word counts, paragraph lengths, photo
density. This file holds the thing the measurements cannot capture, which is how the piece
is actually built. When a draft hits every number and still reads badly, the fault is
almost always in here.

National Geographic remains the model. Not its subject matter and not its tone, but its
**structure**.

---

## 1. The zoom is the whole technique

A good feature narrator changes altitude, repeatedly and on purpose.

**ON THE GROUND.** A person, an object, a thing happening now. Sensory and specific. A
named practitioner doing their work. Dialogue. The surprising physical detail. What the
writer touched, smelled, got wrong, was corrected about.

**AT ALTITUDE.** The explanation. How the thing actually works. History, scale, numbers,
economics, the system. Groundwork and context. What the reader needs in order for the
ground-level scene to mean anything.

Neither altitude survives alone. Twenty paragraphs on the ground is a diary: vivid, and the
reader learns nothing. Twenty at altitude is a textbook: informative, and nobody finishes
it. The article is the **movement between them**.

A piece may climb and descend several times or only once. Once is fine if the single climb
is the spine of the argument. What is never fine is staying at one altitude throughout.

### How to move

The transitions are the craft, and each direction has its own trigger.

**Going up** is triggered by a question the ground just raised. Something was observed that
makes no sense yet. The reader is now asking why, and the climb answers it. This is why you
must put the observation *first*: an explanation that arrives before its question is
lecture, and the reader skims it.

> She told me to leave it there and went back to her bench. Over the next hour it turned
> yellow. Then green. Then a blue. Then purple, and then it stopped.
>
> *(now up)* Air and daylight do that. The gland carries something that becomes the colour
> once air reaches it.

**Coming down** is triggered by the altitude passage reaching a consequence you can point
at. You have just explained a rule; now show it operating on a specific person on a
specific morning.

> *(at altitude)* A snail out of the water begins to rot inside a day, and the gland rots
> with it. So the women have to open them where the boats land them, on the same tide.
>
> *(now down)* A woman cracks the shell with a stone, lifts the gland out with a bronze
> pick, and drops it in a jar. Kuwatta has done this since she was nine.

### The altitude map

The diagnostic. Number the paragraphs, mark each **G** or **A**, and read the column.

- A long run of **G** means the piece is a travelogue. The reader is watching, not learning.
- A long run of **A** means it has become an encyclopaedia entry with a narrator bolted on.
- **G A G A G A** in a strict alternation is its own fault: it reads mechanical and the
  reader feels the machinery. Vary the length of the runs.

Healthy looks like: a short **G** open, a fast climb, a long **A** that lays the
groundwork, a long **G** that spends that groundwork on a scene, and so on, with the runs
different lengths each time.

## 2. Chain, not tour

The commonest structural failure here, and the hardest to see in your own draft.

A **tour** presents eight true things in a row. Each is interesting. None requires the
others. The reader finishes holding facts and no mechanism.

A **chain** makes each fact force the next. The article has a spine you can say aloud.

The purple piece, as a chain:

> the colour is absent from the animal and arrives in air
> → so the gland has to reach air unspoiled
> → so the snails must be opened fresh, where they land
> → so the works sit on the shingle, and so the vats stink, and so the rent falls upwind
> → so the shed stands open to the water, because air is the last free stage
> → and the yield is minute, so eighty thousand animals buy one hem
> → which is why only kings wear it
> → and the workers eat the discard, which is the only reason the labour is affordable

**The reorder test.** Shuffle your sections. If the piece survives, you wrote a tour. In a
chain, moving section four above section two produces visible nonsense.

**The "and therefore" test.** Between any two consecutive sections you should be able to
insert *and therefore* or *which is why*. If the only honest connective is *also*, the
chain is broken at that link.

## 3. Explain the thing. Do not perform explaining it.

The failure mode that produced this document: every paragraph ending on a landed beat
instead of an explanation.

> ✗ Here is where the sums close.
> ✗ That is the whole function, and the function works.
> ✗ Nobility gets added at the far end, by people who were never on this beach.

Each of those is a punchline standing exactly where the teaching should be. They *gesture*
at an insight and leave the reader to reconstruct it, which most readers will not do. The
prose sounds confident and transmits nothing.

> ✓ Eighty thousand animals a day is eighty thousand mouthfuls of meat a day, at no cost to
> anybody. The palace pays these women in grain and figs. It sends down no meat, because the
> work they are already doing produces their protein as waste, at their elbow, before noon.

Longer, plainer, and the reader now owns the idea.

A good line is welcome **after** the explanation has done its work. It is never a
substitute for it. One aphorism per section is a seasoning; one per paragraph is a tic.

---

## 4. Answer the question the reader is actually asking

If the subject is a costly thing, the reader wants to know what it costs. If it is a
dangerous thing, how often people die. If it is a slow thing, how long.

Withholding that number for elegance is a failure, not restraint. An early draft of the
purple piece had Kuwatta say *"nobody counts snails"* and moved on, which felt like a
graceful sidestep and was in fact the article refusing its own job.

The fix used the writer's own competence: Anniwiya weighs and seals goods for the palace,
so when the practitioner will not count, **she** counts. Twenty benches, four thousand
shells each, eighty thousand animals a day, and a day produces one hem.

Where a real figure is genuinely unknown, say so plainly and give the reader the bounds. Do
not hide behind the character's shrug.

---

## 5. The second voice

Every one of these needs a named practitioner who knows more than the writer about this one
thing. They are not decoration and not a source of quotes.

- They **correct** the writer. The writer arrives with an assumption and loses it.
- They make the reader's ignorance safe. Somebody on the page does not know either.
- They supply the working units the palace never records: *a basket colours a hem*.
- They are **uninterested in the article's argument**. Arad-Ea is buying cheap brick.
  Kuwatta is paid whether the cloak means anything or not. The moment the second voice
  starts agreeing with the thesis, they have become a puppet.

The writer is the instrument. The subject is the place and what it makes people eat.

## 6. The shape, start to finish

Not a template to fill in. This is what the shape usually turns out to be.

1. **Open on the ground, mid-action, small.** A person, an object, a problem. No throat
   clearing and no scene-setting. The first line should be a fact, not a frame.
2. **The first climb comes early**, and it states the scale or the stakes. The reader needs
   to know within a few paragraphs why this is worth their time.
3. **The spine section** is the one that explains how the thing works. It is usually the
   longest run at altitude and it is the reason the article exists.
4. **Descend to the human cost.** Who does this work, what it takes out of them, what they
   eat. This is where the site's actual subject lives.
5. **The turn**, where the argument stops being about the mechanism and becomes about the
   people or the value. Short. Earned by everything above it.
6. **Close on the ground, small and concrete.** Never a summary. Never a restatement of the
   thesis. A specific object or gesture that carries the argument without naming it.

The close is worth its own note. Two or three closing beats stacked on each other is the
usual fault: the thesis restated, then a wide statement, then a final image. Cut until one
remains.

---

## 7. Anti-patterns

| Fault | What it looks like | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The tour | Eight true things in a row | Find the chain; reorder-test it |
| Aphorism creep | Paragraphs ending on landed beats | Replace the beat with the explanation |
| The dodge | Refusing the reader's obvious question | Answer it, or state the bounds |
| Altitude lock | Twenty paragraphs at one height | Map G/A and break the run |
| Premature altitude | Explaining before anything raised the question | Move the observation first |
| The puppet | Second voice agreeing with the thesis | Give them their own indifferent motive |
| Stacked closers | Three endings | Keep one |
| Detail without duty | A vivid fact that serves no link in the chain | Cut it or give it a job |

That last one is worth dwelling on. A surprising detail is not automatically earning its
place. Ask what it is **for**: which link in the chain does it carry, or which claim does it
make credible? A detail that answers neither is decoration, however good it is.

---

## 8. Before you call it finished

- Say the chain aloud in one breath. If you cannot, there is no chain.
- Mark every paragraph G or A. Look at the runs.
- Find every paragraph ending on a quip. Replace at least half with explanation.
- Name the reader's obvious question. Confirm the article answers it.
- Check the second voice wants something the article does not.
- Count your closing beats. Keep one.
- **Then** go to `article-pattern.md` and check the numbers.

The order matters. Numbers are a check on a sound piece, not a route to one. A draft that
hits every count and fails this list is worse than one that misses the word target and
holds together.
