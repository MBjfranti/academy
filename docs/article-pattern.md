# The article pattern

> **Read `article-structure.md` first.** This file holds the measured shape: counts,
> lengths, densities. That one holds how the piece is built: the zoom between ground and
> altitude, chain versus tour, explanation versus aphorism. A draft can satisfy every number
> here and still be badly made, and when that happens the fault is always in the other file.

Derived by measuring the eight published articles, not by proposing anything. Where this
contradicts `style.md`, this wins: `style.md` was written before any of these existed and
guessed at numbers the corpus has now settled.

---

## The measured shape

> The word and paragraph counts here **describe the first nine articles as first drafted**.
> The target is now 1,500–2,000 words; see "Length, and what may fill it" below. Everything
> else in this table still holds, because it measures rhythm rather than size.

| | range | aim for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Body words | 824–1273 as drafted | **1,500–2,000** |
| Paragraphs | 25–33 as drafted | **40–52** |
| **Words per paragraph** | 32–41 avg | **~37** |
| **Words per sentence** | 9.2–10.9 avg | **~10** |
| Longest sentence in a piece | 22–31 | **under 30** |
| Headings | 3–5 | **4** |
| Images including hero | 4–8 as drafted | **9–10** |
| Pull quotes | 1 (one piece has 2) | **1** |
| Glossary terms | 2–5 | **3** |
| Standing paragraphs | 3–6 | **3** |
| Paragraphs containing speech | 0–5 | **1–2** |

**A ten-word average sentence is the single hardest number here**, and it is the one that
makes the prose sound like this site. `style.md` asked for 80-word paragraphs of three to
five sentences. The corpus runs **37-word paragraphs of three or four ten-word sentences.**
Half the length, twice the force.

## Every article is an investigation, not a tour

One governing claim, stated in the standfirst, tested through the body, and left honestly
narrowed rather than proved.

| Article | The claim being tested |
| --- | --- |
| The Name of the Broth | A legible word whose meaning is gone. Cooking cannot recover it. |
| The Sea Goes One Way | The sea overrides the captain's stated plan. |
| The Thousand Cups of Pylos | The room decides the cup, not the drinker. |
| The Iliad and the Honesty | The clay keeps the ration; the song keeps the feeling. |
| Four Days Out of Ugarit | Distance prices everything it carries. |

The writer **does something** and reports what happened. Balāṭu runs two pots with one
variable changed. Yadinu walks the cargo every morning with a tablet and a nose. Anniwiya
counts oil into a feast she is not invited to. Nobody stands and explains.

**The test has a control.** "I used the same lamb, fat, water, salt, onion, leek, garlic,
cumin, coriander, and mint in both pots. Only the pulse changed."

## The parts

**Title.** Title case, four to six words, opening with "The" six times out of eight. It names
the governing fact, not the location: *The Name of the Broth*, *The Sea Goes One Way*, *The
God Who Ate Four Times a Day*. Concrete and slightly withholding. A place name only earns a
slot when it is doing image work, as in *The Thousand Cups of Pylos*.

**Standfirst.** Two or three short sentences. A setup, then the fact that cuts across it.

> The singer made our summer raids last ten years. He called twelve hundred ships to one
> beach and set a beautiful woman at the centre. The scale is false. The hunger is honest.

**Opening line.** Straight into a concrete event or a flat provocation. No scene-setting, no
throat-clearing, no orientation.

> The sign is clear. That is the irritating part.
> Marduk ate four times each day and never praised the cook.
> Four days out of Ugarit, the donkey carrying our bronze bells sat down in the road.

**Headings.** Three to five, sentence case, three to five words, and each one **makes a
claim** rather than labelling a section: *A word with no kitchen. The hold argues back. The
room decides the cup. Water makes the distance.*

**The second person.** Always there, named, holding a real position, given direct speech with
plain tags. They are characterised by behaviour, not description: *"He ate a second bowl of
the beans while explaining why the chickpeas had won."* They are allowed the last word.

**Ending.** Short, flat, and it lands the claim without restating it. Often it turns on the
writer's own character.

> He said we had arrived exactly as planned. I wrote the date on my tablet. The sea had kept
> the better account.

> The cup has done everything asked of it. I tell him it is still bad. A king's daughter
> learns expensive tastes before she learns useful ones.

**Pull quote.** Exactly one, and it is a compressed claim rather than a nice line: *"Dried
curd is a herd reduced to stones."*

**Standing.** Three short paragraphs, in order: what is attested, what is inferred, what is
invented. The last one always names the invented people.

**Glossary.** Two to five terms, one flat line each. It exists so the prose never stops.

---

## Captions

**"The author" — third person, every time.** Present tense. One or two short sentences. The
first states the action; the second, when there is one, adds the detail that makes it a
photograph rather than a diagram.

> The author opens the pot. His assistant has already chosen his evidence.
> The author breaks bread while her son ties a sandal and her grandson reaches across the basket.
> The author contests the measure while the scribe checks his ostracon.

Where the writer is absent the caption states the fact plainly, and may carry the argument:

> Headland follows headland through smoke, salt haze, and morning glare.
> The wedges remain precise. The ingredient does not.
> One fire, two pots, and an argument with no neutral spoon.

A caption never editorialises and never explains the joke.

## The visual brief

`visual-brief.js` sits beside each article under the rule **"Choose the final image before
writing its caption."** Four fields per frame:

- `role` — hero or body
- `claim` — what this picture must argue
- `preferredEvidence` — the specific scene that argues it
- `avoid` — the named cliché it would otherwise become

Separating `claim` from `preferredEvidence` is the whole point, and it is what stops a scene
description bloating: the claim keeps the picture honest, the evidence keeps it concrete, and
`avoid` names the failure in advance.

## What I was getting wrong

Recorded because these were my own habits and they will come back.

**I taught the reader.** Map lessons, "you dig it as Ras Shamra", period explainers, the
distance from here to the pyramids. The published articles trust the reader completely and
let the glossary carry the names. Orientation belongs in the apparatus, never in the prose.

**I wrote tours.** Arrive, look around, describe, reach a dish. These articles pose a
question and test it. The difference shows in the first line: a tour opens on a place, an
investigation opens on a problem.

**My headings were labels.** "The anatomy of the plate" names a section. "The hold argues
back" makes a claim.

**My sentences were half again too long.** I averaged fifteen to eighteen words against the
house ten, and I stacked clauses to get there.

**My captions were asides.** They commented on the picture. These describe what is happening
in it and let the second sentence do the human work.

**I over-explained the writer's feelings.** The published pieces show the writer working and
being corrected, and let the reader draw it. Anniwiya's whole history arrives in one line:
*"I was born beautiful in a king's house."*

## Length, and what may fill it

**1,500 to 2,000 words.** This supersedes the ~1,000-word shape measured from the first nine
articles, which was a description of what existed rather than a target.

The extra length is not licence to run long. It is licence to **finish the explanations the
short version skipped**, and it is spent on exactly three things:

**1. Research that is already in `sources.md` and never reached the body.** This is the
richest seam in the corpus and the cheapest to mine. Several articles did real work, cited
it, and then asserted the conclusion in one line. If the evidence spine names a tablet
series, a ration figure, a wreck manifest or a frontier instruction, the reader should meet
it in the prose.

**2. The second voice.** Every piece needs somebody who answers back, holds a position of
their own, and is right often enough not to be a foil. Several of the first nine gave that
person four lines and a good exit. One gave them nothing at all. A real interlocutor is
information: they know a trade the writer does not.

**3. Explanation an outsider needs.** See the section below on reading the writer. A claim
asserted is a claim the reader has to take on trust; a claim worked through is one they can
carry.

**Nothing else.** No scene-setting, no restatement, no atmosphere for its own sake. If an
added paragraph does not carry a fact, a voice, or a working-out, it is padding and it fails
the brief.

## Length is where personality lives

The short articles were not just thin on information. They were thin on **people**, and the
writer was the first person squeezed out.

A thousand words of investigation leaves room for the claim and nothing else, so the writer
becomes a lens. Two thousand leaves room for the writer to be a person with a trade, a
history, a temper and an appetite. **That is the reason for the length, and it is the first
thing to protect when expanding.**

Practically, every expansion adds writer as well as evidence:

| Writer | What the extra room is for |
| --- | --- |
| **Yadinu** | He counts. He is a provisioning scribe who walked out of the job, and he still cannot pass a market without totalling it. Expansion means he does the arithmetic on the page, out loud, and is delighted when it comes out strange. He is always inside a crew: `we`, not `I`. |
| **Henut** | She is funny every third paragraph, and the jokes come from watching people be people. She is physically present, has appetite, and reaches for the bakery when she needs a comparison. Expansion means more of her and more of her trade, never a longer solemn description. |
| **Balāṭu** | He argues, and he argues with somebody. Nabû-ēṭir exists so the argument has two sides. Expansion means the disagreement runs longer and the evidence gets heavier, not that the conclusion gets restated. |
| **Anniwiya** | She is structurally alone: the highest `I` and the lowest `we` on the site. Expansion means more of what she alone noticed, and more of the two lives she can compare, never a chorus. |

The failure mode to watch for is the writer thinning out as the article grows: more evidence,
more scene, and the person who was supposed to be telling it receding into a voice-over. If
a draft grows by four hundred words and the writer appears in none of them, the draft is
wrong however good the new facts are.

## Read the writer before writing the writer

`docs/personas.md` describes a voice. It does not let you hear one, and a draft written from
the description alone comes out voiceless in a specific and repeatable way.

This happened on `the-geese-of-the-delta`. The spec says Henut is "warm, funny and borderline
goofy, and this is the most important note about her." The first draft gave both good lines
to her interviewee and left Henut a solemn note-taker, because the description says *that she
is funny* without showing *how*.

**So read the writer's own published article first, and steal the cadence.** Henut in
`lifetimes-in-the-reign-of-ramesses-ii` lands a joke every third paragraph, always by
watching people be people: a scribe with "a strong belief in his own thumb"; a village that
cheers a royal jubilee and then asks whether the extra beer will arrive before sunset; a man
who stayed off work after a beating from his wife, on which she notes only that she would
like the wife's account and that the scribe lacked the courage to ask.

Three things carried over once that was read properly, and all three were absent before:

**Sentence variance is the whole engine.** Her published cadence alternates four-word punches
with long warm enumerations: "knees, marriages, apprentices, missing teeth, and the names
called at a ration window." The first draft ran flat at eight to twelve words with a standard
deviation near 3, and read curt and wrought at every length. Raising the deviation to about
5.5 fixed the tone without changing a single opinion in the piece. **A uniform sentence
length reads as coldness no matter what the words say.**

**She explains systems to an outsider,** patiently and at length: where grain begins, who
carries it, what it crosses, so that "every loaf in the village has already made a journey."
A passage that asserts without explaining reads as wrought. Compare the two hippopotamus
drafts: "it needs no reason to be angry" says nothing, where "the crocodile is the one in the
stories, the hippopotamus is the one that ruins a village" teaches and is funnier.

**The register lives in the writer's own trade.** Henut measures the world in bakery: a
number given straight off is one she would give for the weight of a sack of emmer, and a
mother's flat refusal gets compared to a scribe proposing they manage with one oven. Reach
for the writer's job when you need a simile, never for a general-purpose one.

This is why the corpus wins over the spec, everywhere the two disagree.

## The photography

Two notes from the commissioning session set the whole register, and both are still binding:

> I want the photography to read much more national geographic... unstaged... artistic at
> times, some motion blur, realistic... unique, kind of surprising.

And, rejecting a landscape that was technically clean and photographically dead:

> the image is a bit too textured and clear... even without modern pollution, smoke, haze
> and sea mist would give that kind of image more depth... and the nat geo style wouldn't
> have the whole thing in focus?

That second note is the more useful of the two. **Nothing should be entirely in focus, and
atmosphere is what creates depth.** Compare `the-name-of-the-broth/two-pots.webp`, the best
frame on the site: a body crosses the foreground in motion blur, the herbs in front are
gone, and only two men and the steam between them are sharp.

### The five National Geographic rules

These are the whole style. Everything below is machinery for getting them into a prompt.

1. **Nothing is entirely in focus.** One depth is sharp. Everything nearer and further
   falls away. A frame that is uniformly crisp reads as a render.
2. **Atmosphere carries depth.** Dust, smoke, haze, steam, sea mist. A clean air column is
   what makes a wide shot look like a diorama.
3. **Something blocks the foreground**, and it must be named specifically: a sack rim, an
   elbow, a parapet, a basket. This kills the miniature effect more reliably than any
   negative prompt.
4. **The camera has a position in the room**, not a lens spec. "From inside the queue at
   shoulder height" produces reportage. "85mm f/1.4" produces a product shot.
5. **Unstaged.** Nobody looks at the camera, nobody poses, nobody performs an emotion.
   People are caught mid-action, mid-gesture, slightly bored. Motion blur on anyone moving.

### Photo density, and the paired frame

Measured against a National Geographic feature of comparable length, the first drafts ran at
roughly **half** the photo density: about 300 body words per image against their 173. Ten
images per article, hero included, puts this site in the same band at about 150.

**The paired frame** is the layout worth stealing. Two photographs are set to one **matched
height** and their widths are left alone, so each keeps its own aspect ratio, in a band that
breaks outside the reading measure:

```
{ at: 12, name: 'copper-ingot', placeholder: true,
  pair: [ { name: 'copper-ingot', ratio: '3 / 2' },
          { name: 'tin-ingot',    ratio: '4 / 5' } ] }
```

Normalising the **height** rather than the width is the whole trick. Match the widths instead
and a portrait becomes enormous next to a landscape strip. The band runs to 1180px against a
672px text column, because the break is the point.

Use it where two things are genuinely a pair and the comparison is the content: the two
soaked pulses in `the-name-of-the-broth`, the copper and the tin in `the-sea-goes-one-way`.

### Planned frames render as empty boxes

A figure marked `placeholder: true` draws a dashed hole at its own `ratio` with its `name`
printed in it, and no caption. This lets an article be laid out and its spacing judged before
any photograph exists.

**A placeholder carries no caption and no alt text**, which is the caption rule doing its
job: the final image is chosen before its caption is written. The `name` is the brief. When
the frame is shot, drop `placeholder`, add `alt` and `caption`, and the box becomes a
picture.

### Paragraph spacing

Body paragraphs are separated by **one full blank line**: the margin equals the line-height.
This was 1.15em against a 1.7 line-height, which is about two thirds of a line, and the
column read grey and crowded. National Geographic runs 18px type on a 32px line with a 32px
paragraph gap, so their page sits on a single 32px rhythm. Ours now does the same.

No indents anywhere. The measure lives on the container rather than on each paragraph, so a
centred column of left-ragged type sits on the same axis as the headline above it.

### The National Geographic register is a floor, not a formula

The first version of this section prescribed one recipe — available light, shallow depth of
field, motion blur, imperfect framing — and applied it to every frame in every article. That
is wrong about the thing it is imitating. **National Geographic runs several distinct
photographic modes.** What they share is that the picture is good, not that it was taken the
same way.

Applying one recipe to ten frames produces ten versions of one photograph. The evidence is in
this corpus: the two best images on the site, `reed-birds` and `smoke-share`, are both
silhouettes, and both only exist because the generic block was overridden for them. Under the
standard rules neither should have been shot that way.

So the spec is now a **floor** every frame must clear, and a **mode** each frame chooses.

#### The floor

Non-negotiable, whatever the mode:

- **The light is doing something.** A direction, a single source, a backlight, a hard edge.
  Flat even illumination is the one universal failure, and it is what makes a frame read as
  an illustration.
- **One subject, and the eye knows where to go.** Whether by focus, by contrast or by shape.
- **Nothing is arranged for the camera.** Nobody looks down the lens, nobody poses, nobody
  performs an emotion.
- **Real texture.** Dust, grease, wear, sweat, smoke, weather.
- **It earns its place.** The picture shows something the prose cannot, or it is decoration.

#### The modes

Pick one per frame, and vary them across an article. A brief may name it in a `mode` field.

**`reportage`** — the working default. Available light, one plane sharp and the rest falling
away, motion blur on somebody moving, a body cropped by the frame edge, foreground mass close
to the lens. For process, crowds, hands, a thing being done. *Examples: `ration-tally`,
`liver-skewers`, the Delta `hero`.*

**`silhouette`** — the subject collapses to shape against a bright ground: fire, sky, dust,
smoke, water, a lit doorway, a pale wall. Detail goes; outline carries everything. Extreme
contrast, blown highlights, near-black subject. Shallow focus and motion blur are *optional
here and often wrong* — a silhouette wants edge, not softness. For mass, atmosphere, weather,
a figure against a place. *Examples: `reed-birds`, `smoke-share`.*

**`specimen`** — one object, given room, lit to be read. Centred or near it, still, clean
ground, no reportage grammar at all. The Nat Geo feature this site was measured against opens
with exactly this: a bead dress on a stand, full height, nothing else in the frame. For an
artefact that is the article's subject: a tablet, a cup, a jar, a tool. *Example: the
`allocation-tablet` half of the Pylos pair.*

**`portrait`** — a person, still, at conversational distance, looking away or at their work.
Sharper and more composed than reportage. Half the face in shadow is usually the whole shot.
*Example: `commander`.*

**`landscape`** — the country is the subject and a figure is small in it, or absent. This was
previously banned outright, on the grounds that a small figure in a wide landscape reads as a
diorama. That is true of a *flat* wide landscape. With hard directional light, real
atmosphere and a foreground that means something, it is a mode rather than a mistake. Use it
sparingly and never for a scene about people. *Example: `road-west`.*

#### Vary the mode across an article

Ten frames in one mode is the same fault as ten frames of one composition. A good set runs
something like: a `reportage` hero, two or three more `reportage` in the body, one
`silhouette`, one `specimen` for whatever object the piece turns on, one `portrait` of the
second voice. The `pair` layout is a good place to put a `specimen` beside a `reportage`,
because the contrast between the two modes is part of what makes the pairing work.

#### The block, per mode

`Style/medium` still takes a verbatim block. Which one depends on the mode.

**reportage:**

> unstaged documentary reportage in the National Geographic register, shot on location on a
> 35mm prime in available light. STRONG DIRECTIONAL LIGHT with real contrast and deep shadow.
> SHALLOW depth of field, one plane sharp and everything else falling away soft. MOTION BLUR
> on at least one moving person. Imperfect framing: subject off centre, a body cropped by the
> frame edge, figures overlapping. Real grime and wear. Fine film grain. Nobody looks at the
> camera. A real photograph, never a painting, a render or a clean illustration.

**silhouette:**

> National Geographic silhouette photography. The subject is BACKLIT against a bright ground
> and reads as a dark shape with a rimmed edge, detail collapsing into outline. Extreme
> contrast: the ground blown to white, the subject near black. Shape and gesture carry the
> frame, not surface detail. Keep the edges crisp. Fine film grain. A real photograph, never a
> painting, a render or a clean illustration.

**specimen:**

> National Geographic object photography. One object, isolated and given room, lit to be read
> rather than dramatised: a single raking source that rakes the surface and describes its
> texture, on a plain uncluttered ground that falls away dark. The object is sharp end to end
> and fills the frame. Still, deliberate, no motion. A real photograph of a real object, never
> a painting, a render, a museum display case or a catalogue cut-out.

### The model

`gpt-image-2`, called directly through the OpenAI API.

The published site images were made inside the Codex session through its built-in image
tool, which does not record the model in the transcript. Two things independently point at
`gpt-image-2`: the session's own tooling notes state that its CLI path defaults to it, and
the site owner confirmed it as the model he had found and used. Treat it as settled.

Two traps, both hit in practice:

- `gpt-image-1` produces the amber, plasticky, video-game look this site spent a long time
  getting out of. Do not fall back to it.
- `gpt-image-2` **rejects `input_fidelity`**. Passing it returns a 400. Identity is held by
  the `Input image` field's wording instead.

Reference images go through `images.edit()`; frames with no writer in them go through
`images.generate()`.

### The prompt template

**Nine labelled fields, in this order.** Not a paragraph of prose, and not the seven-field
version this file carried previously, which dropped `Lighting/mood`, `Period accuracy` and
`Constraints`. Those three omissions are what produced the amber wash, the costume drama and
the diorama look, every time.

```
Use case:            historical-scene
Asset type:          editorial photograph for the report "<title>"
Input image:         identity and costume reference for <writer>; preserve the same
                     <man/woman>, face, age, colouring, hair, <the full garment list from
                     personas.md>. Do not reproduce the composition of the reference image.
Primary request:     <the action, including the small human beat>
Scene/backdrop:      <place and what must be visible at the edges>; the lived-in world of
                     about 1226 BC, never ruins and never an excavation
Composition/framing: cinematic 3:2 landscape (or 2:3 portrait); horizon level; <camera
                     position in the room>; <what blocks the foreground>; <subject> on the
                     right third, caught mid-gesture
Lighting/mood:       <the light the article actually states>; <palette>; <mood>; NOT golden
                     hour, NOT orange, NOT warm low sun
Style/medium:        <the block for this frame's MODE, verbatim>
Period accuracy:     bronze rather than iron; handwoven wool and linen; mud brick, timber
                     and stone; no classical Greek or Roman architecture, no plate armour,
                     no modern objects, no text, logo or watermark
Constraints:         <the visual brief's `rule`>; everything is life size, with no diorama
                     or miniature effect; hands anatomically natural
Avoid:               <the frame's `avoid` field, verbatim from visual-brief.js>
```

`Style/medium` and `Period accuracy` are constants. Copy them as written. The other seven
fields are per frame.

### Dress everybody, not just the writer

`personas.md` specifies the writer's costume down to the stripe on the shawl, so the writer
comes back right. **Everyone else in the frame defaults to sword-and-sandal.** The first
`ration-tally` frame for `guests-on-the-lukka-road` returned Hittite infantry in segmented
banded armour with crested Roman helmets, from a prompt whose `Period accuracy` line already
said "no plate armour".

A generic noun invites the cliché. "Two waiting soldiers" gets you a costume drama; "two
soldiers in dull undyed wool tunics, soft conical caps and no armour" gets you the Late
Bronze Age. Name the dress of every group that appears, in `Primary request`, at the moment
you name the group.

The negatives that earn their place in `Period accuracy`, because the model reaches for them
unprompted:

> no Roman segmented or banded armour, no lorica, no crested or plumed helmets, no muscled
> cuirass, no classical Greek or Roman architecture, no hoplite equipment

### Lighting overrides the persona default

`docs/personas.md` gives each writer a house light, and Yadinu's reads **"Light: golden. Low
sun."** That line is the single largest source of the amber wash, because it is true of a
quay at evening and false of everything else he writes about.

**The article's stated time of day wins.** If the prose says the column arrived at the
hottest part of the day, the frame is hard flat noon, bleached and dusty. Write the negation
explicitly into `Lighting/mood` — `NOT golden hour, NOT orange, NOT warm low sun` — because
the model reverts to amber without it.

Vary the light across the five frames of one article. A set that is uniformly warm looks
like a set, and a set that is uniformly warm and wide looks like a diorama.

### The brief drives the prompt

`visual-brief.js` is not documentation. Two of its fields load directly into the template:

| brief field | prompt field |
| --- | --- |
| `mode` (per frame) | which `Style/medium` block, and how `Lighting/mood` is written |
| `rule` (per article) | `Constraints` |
| `avoid` (per frame) | `Avoid` |
| `preferredEvidence` | `Primary request` |
| `claim` | what the frame has to prove, and what the caption may say |

A generator that composes prompts without reading the brief is doing it wrong. That is how
the first pass of `guests-on-the-lukka-road` was produced, and every one of its five `avoid`
lines went unused.

### Identity across a set

Every frame with a writer in it is generated through `images.edit()` with that writer's
portrait passed as the reference image. The file is always:

```
public/img/writers/<writer>/face.webp
```

One canonical face per writer, reused across every article they write. That file is the only
thing holding a writer's appearance stable across nine reports, so it is never regenerated
casually. A frame with no writer in it takes no reference and goes through
`images.generate()`.

The reference is passed with **"Do not reproduce the composition of the reference image."**
The reference holds the face; the prohibition stops a reference-conditioned set collapsing
into five versions of the same shot.

The `Input image` field names the costume in full, from `personas.md`, not just the face.
Yadinu is identifiable at forty pixels by the pale shawl over the **left** shoulder, so the
shawl is in the field every time.

### Print the prompt before spending

A generator that composes prompts from constants can drop one silently, and the failure is
invisible until the images come back wrong.

This happened on `guests-on-the-lukka-road`. The Hittite costume block was interpolated into the
frame descriptions with an f-string, and in Python implicit string concatenation only the
fragment carrying the `f` prefix interpolates. The rest stay literal. One frame sent the text
`{HITTITE_MEN}` to the API as a meaningless token and the other lost the block entirely. Both
came back in Roman armour, and the second pass was worse than the first.

So, before any generation run: assemble every prompt, assert that no placeholder survives,
and grep the assembled text for the two or three phrases that must be present. It costs
nothing and it catches the whole class of fault.

```
for n in FRAMES:
    p = prompt_for(n)
    assert "{" not in p                     # nothing unsubstituted
    print(n, "CLEAN-SHAVEN" in p, "STRONG DIRECTIONAL" in p)
```

### Candidates, then selection

Frames are generated as candidates and one is chosen. The file convention, visible in every
shipped article directory:

```
public/img/reports/<slug>/<name>-candidate-01.png     every generation, kept
public/img/reports/<slug>/<name>-candidate-02-<why>.png   a named variation
public/img/reports/<slug>/<name>.webp                 the chosen frame
public/img/reports/<slug>/hero-thumb.webp             the hero only
```

`four-days-out-of-ugarit/first-ridge` carries three candidates suffixed `-atmosphere` and
`-documentary`. Name a variation after the thing being varied, so the folder records why a
frame was reshot rather than just that it was.

Candidates are never deleted. They are the record of what the prompt actually did, and they
are the only way to tell a prompt fault from a model fault on the next article.

## The caption rule, and where it came from

> the images and the captions are at odds

That single observation produced the rule now heading every `visual-brief.js`:

> **Choose the final image before writing its caption.**

With two consequences that were adopted as pipeline law:

- Captions cannot enter the pipeline before an image is selected.
- No provisional images or captions attached to new articles.

This is why every published caption describes what is actually in the frame. It is also why
the article directories were restructured: an image can be commissioned against a specific
claim instead of pulled from a shared narrator folder.

## The rule that reverses my earlier docs

> in the same way Henut doesn't know about R.II's death, why does Anniwiya know about
> Homer? She should know about the poems being performed... and as someone who was married
> across the Aegean and then captured back, hearing that song should be the "text" of her
> knowledge? She can posit that maybe it's been told over and over, since it's clearly
> popular and at least a few decades old at 1226.

And, rejecting a line that addressed the modern reader:

> the "You" which is accusatory of OUR time, but the author was Homer, who is MUCH closer
> to her time.

**The writers live in 1226 BC and do not know the future.** They are not spirits, they do
not know your archaeology, and they never address a modern reader. Anniwiya knows a song
she has heard performed, not a poem written down five centuries after her. Henut does not
know that Ramesses dies.

This **supersedes `voice.md` §1–§6 and the corresponding sections of `personas.md`**, which
describe a narrator who is "of that world but not in it any more" and who "knows your
archaeology". That conceit was mine, and it is gone. Those files are marked accordingly.

The proof is in the corpus. Across eight articles the second person appears 0.5–1.0 times
per thousand words, and nearly every instance is dialogue (*"Your apprentice?" I asked*) or
impersonal (*the meal tells you the route*). No article names an excavator, a decipherment
or a date in our era.

**One residue worth knowing about.** `the-name-of-the-broth` still says "one modern reading"
and "the published arguments", and Balāṭu is the writer most likely to earn that licence
because textual dispute is his subject. It is unresolved rather than settled.

## The other standing rules

**Bylined first person.** "I do want these 4 writers as my WRITERS, with bylines, so
self-written 3rd person is out." The writer says "I" in the prose and is called "the author"
in the captions.

**No reconstruction language in captions.** "REMOVE the reconstruction language from the
captions sitewide... this is a fictional site, and doesn't need to be called out as such in
that way." Honesty about evidence lives in `standing` and `sources.md`. A caption states
what is in the frame and nothing else.

**Henut says Pharaoh, never king.**

**Egypt is not all desert.** "Don't be afraid to add green plants and life to Egypt, it's
not ALL dust and desert."

**Image placement need not be literal.** "It can be a little off. An image can precede its
discussion, that builds expectation, or callback if the opposite." What is not allowed is
clumping: a long run with no image and then two at the end.

**No inset figures anywhere.** Only `hero`, `col` and `wide`. Images are spaced through the
piece, and the article must carry enough text to justify how many it has. The count scales
with the length.

**Candidates are PNG and gitignored. Only the chosen WebP is committed.**

## Working practice worth keeping

Frames are generated as **numbered candidates** kept beside the final asset —
`hero-candidate-01.png`, `hero-candidate-02-documentary.png`, `first-ridge-candidate-03-documentary.png`
— with the variant approach in the filename. The PNGs are gitignored; only the chosen WebP
is committed. Rejections stay on disk and stay comparable.
