# What I think a National Geographic feature is

**Written before receiving the three briefs.** The point of writing it first is that I
cannot afterwards adjust the definition to flatter whatever I produce. Everything below is
a claim that real articles can prove wrong, and the second half of the exercise is finding
out which parts are wrong.

---

## Part 1: The commitments

### 1. The photographer is a co-author, not an illustrator

I am committing to this as the single most distinctive fact about the form. Many features
are assigned as photo stories first and written second. The pictures carry a parallel
narrative rather than decorating the text, captions carry information the body never states,
and the photographer is credited beside the writer.

**Therefore:** for each brief I will produce a photo edit as a deliberate act, not a list of
illustrations. Each frame gets a job that the prose does not do.

### 2. Wonder is the default register

Not outrage, not irony, not argument. Curiosity and awe, sustained even over grim subject
matter. A Geographic piece about a collapsing fishery is still, in its bones, amazed by the
fish.

This is the commitment I am least confident I can execute, because my instinct runs to
argument and dry irony. I predict this is where my drafts will diverge most.

### 3. The access is part of the story

Somebody went somewhere difficult. Time spent, permits, seasons waited out, the dive, the
climb, the eleven months. The difficulty of getting the material is itself reported.

### 4. Mechanism gets explained in full

How the thing actually works, in plain language, at length, usually built from an expert's
explanation plus the writer's restatement. Geographic does not gesture at science and move
on. It teaches.

### 5. Attribution is constant

Real named people with ages, jobs and institutions. "says", "according to", "who has
studied X for two decades". The writer's authority is borrowed and shown to be borrowed,
rather than asserted.

### 6. The local expert ranks with the credentialled one

The herder, the fisher, the guide, the grandmother. Given the same weight as the visiting
scientist, and often allowed to be right where the scientist is wrong.

### 7. Deep time against a human scene

The characteristic move: a person doing something ordinary this morning, set against sixty
million years, or an ice core, or a migration older than agriculture.

### 8. Specificity as a moral position

Latin binomials. Exact place names. Real numbers with sources. The count of the colony. The
depth in metres. Vagueness reads as not having gone.

### 9. Structure

- **Open** in scene, close, mid-action, on a named person. Not on the thesis.
- **The nut graf** arrives within the first several paragraphs and states what this is about
  and why now.
- **The zoom** thereafter: ground level and altitude, alternating in runs of uneven length.
- **A "why now" peg.** A discovery, a threat, an anniversary, a change.
- **Close** on a person, usually small and concrete, often unresolved rather than summarised.

### 10. Sentences are journalism, not literature

Clean, conventional, unshowy. Serving information. The prose does not want to be noticed.

## Part 2: Falsifiable predictions

Numbers I expect to find when I check real articles. Recorded so the comparison has teeth.

| # | Prediction |
| --- | --- |
| P1 | Print feature length runs 3,000–6,000 words, well above the 1,500–2,000 I write |
| P2 | 20–35% of paragraphs contain a direct quotation |
| P3 | 5–12 named individuals per feature, most with age and occupation given |
| P4 | The writer's "I" appears, but in under 10% of paragraphs, and often not at all |
| P5 | The opening 150 words contain a named person and a physical action, and no thesis |
| P6 | A nut graf appears between paragraphs 3 and 10 |
| P7 | Present tense for scenes, past tense for history, switching without comment |
| P8 | At least one paragraph of pure natural-history or scientific explanation over 100 words |
| P9 | Subheads present online, rare or absent in print layout |
| P10 | The final paragraph returns to a person or place from the opening third |
| P11 | Aphorism density is far lower than mine: under one memorable-line-per-section |
| P12 | Sentences average 18–25 words, well above the ~10 I have been writing for this site |

## Part 3: Where I predict my drafts will fail

Stated in advance, because predicting my own failure modes is the only way to know whether
the comparison taught me anything.

1. **Too short.** I will write 1,800 words where the form wants 4,000.
2. **Too aphoristic.** I end paragraphs on landed beats. Geographic ends them on facts.
3. **Too few quotes.** I paraphrase people instead of letting them talk. I expect real
   features to be far more quote-dense than anything I produce unprompted.
4. **Too argumentative.** I will build a thesis and drive it. The form is more often
   exploratory, and content to leave a contradiction standing.
5. **Too tidy.** Real reporting has loose ends, sources who disagree, questions left open. I
   will resolve everything.
6. **Under-described place.** I am spare about landscape. Geographic is lush about it.
7. **Too few named people.** I will use one second voice where the form uses eight.
8. **No "why now".** My instinct is timeless-essay. The form is pegged.
9. **Compression mistaken for craft.** My sentences will be half the length they should be,
   and I will believe that is a virtue when here it is a tic.

---

## Part 4: What I will produce per brief

1. A feature written to the commitments above.
2. A photo edit: 8–12 frames, each with the assignment, the technique, and the job it does
   that the text does not.
3. A short note on which commitments I found myself fighting.

Then I look up the real thing, and mark my own paper.
