# Costume: Hatti, c. 1226 BC

Written because the images kept coming back generic. Given "two waiting soldiers", the model
produces Roman segmented armour and crested helmets. Given "a Hittite officer", it produces a
bearded biblical extra. Neither is Hittite, and neither is close.

This file is the evidence and the prompt block that comes out of it. It is written for
`region: 'hatti'`. The other three regions need their own and do not have one yet.

## The two markers that do the work

A frame reads as Hittite or it does not, and two details decide it.

**Beardless, with a long pigtail.** Hittite men in both Hittite and Egyptian depiction are
clean-shaven and wear their hair long, gathered into a single thick plait down the back. The
Egyptians used exactly this to tell Hittites apart from the bearded Semitic peoples in the
same relief. Every bearded soldier in a frame is a defect.

**Upturned, pointed-toe shoes.** The single most diagnostic Hittite object. The 1911
Britannica entry, still quoted for this because the observation has held, notes the upturned
shoe "is not found on other Asiatic monuments, except where Hittites are portrayed." Put it
in every frame with feet in it.

Nothing else is as cheap or as decisive.

## The evidence

**Yazılıkaya, Chamber B, the twelve running gods** (`refs/hittite/yazilikaya-twelve-gods.jpg`,
13th century BC, so contemporary). The canonical silhouette, twelve times in a row: tall
pointed conical caps, short belted kilts ending above the knee, sickle swords carried up over
the shoulder, and unmistakable upturned pointed toes on every figure. If a generated frame
does not share this silhouette, it is wrong.

**The King's Gate warrior, Ḫattuša** (`refs/hittite/kings-gate-warrior.jpg`). A short,
richly patterned wrap-around kilt, a horned helmet, an axe, and a crescent sword at the belt.
Note the horns: in Hittite religion horns on a helmet mark a **god**, which is why this figure
is read as one. Horns never go on a mortal soldier.

**The Şapinuva helmet.** A real conical bronze helmet from a 13th-century BC destruction
layer, sharp crown, rivet holes and extended flaps covering cheeks, ears and neck. **No crest
and no plume.** Hittite `huprushhi`; Hurrian `gur-sip-pi`.

**Infantry on campaign carried very little armour**, plausibly because they walked a long way
to fight: a leather helmet with cheek and neck flaps, a leather-over-wood shield, a short
stabbing sword or dagger, and a thrusting spear. Three-quarter-length bronze scale armour
belongs to the chariot crews and the elite, not to men on a road.

That last point matters for this site. A provincial detachment walking the Lower Land road is
the lightly equipped case, not the scale-armour case.

## The prompt block

Paste into `Primary request` at the moment a group of Hittite men is named. Naming the group
without it is what produces the costume drama.

> Hittite Anatolian men: CLEAN-SHAVEN, no beard and no moustache, black hair worn long and
> gathered into one thick pigtail down the back. Short belted wool kilts to mid-thigh, or
> knee-length belted tunics. Soft conical felt caps, or plain conical leather helmets with a
> sharp pointed crown and flaps covering the ears, cheeks and neck: NO crest, NO plume, NO
> horns. Shoes with distinctly UPTURNED POINTED TOES. Thrusting spears and small
> leather-over-wood shields; a short bronze dagger at the belt. NO body armour of any kind on
> marching infantry.

An officer may carry a bronze sickle sword with a crescent blade. Scale armour is available
for a chariot crew and for nobody else.

And in `Period accuracy`, because the model reaches for these unprompted:

> no Roman segmented or banded armour, no lorica, no crested or plumed helmets, no muscled
> cuirass, no hoplite equipment, no classical Greek or Roman architecture

## What is not settled

The pigtail is firmer in Egyptian depiction than in Hittite sculpture, where hair is often
just long. Both are defensible; the plait is the one that reads at a glance.

Sources of this period describe Hittites through Egyptian eyes, and the older literature
carries nineteenth-century racial typology alongside the costume observation. The clothing,
hair and beard details above are corroborated across sources. The physiognomic claims that
travel with them in the older entries are not used here and should not enter a prompt.
