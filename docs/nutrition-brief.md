# Nutrition brief

**Status: reference only. Nothing here is implemented.** The shipped site
computes a per-serving panel for the Four bases (`src/data/nutrition.js`) and
says nothing about the wider question. This is the thinking for when it does.

---

## The question, stated precisely

Not *"was life in 1300 BC nutritionally better than today?"* — obviously not.
The controlled question is:

> **What happens nutritionally if a modern person eats a varied diet restricted
> largely to foods available in the eastern Mediterranean and Near East,
> c. 1750–1150 BC?**

The food comes from a supermarket. The milk is pasteurised, the lamb inspected,
the grain not mouldy. There is refrigeration, clean water and year-round
availability.

### Modern provisioning assumptions — state these on the page

- Modern food safety, refrigeration and clean water
- Year-round supermarket availability
- Adequate calorie supply, no scarcity
- Modern cooking equipment permitted
- Historically appropriate ingredients, historically plausible preparations preferred
- Quantities measured by modern nutritional methods

**Food scarcity, spoilage, parasites and contaminated water are deliberately
excluded from the scorecard.** They were real and they dominated Bronze Age
life, but they are facts about living conditions, not about the food system —
and this experiment evaluates the food system. They belong on a separate page,
*"What this experiment isn't."*

---

## Two experiments — we are doing A

**A · Ancient ingredient constraint.** Anything whose *food identity* would have
been recognisable in 1300 BC is allowed in its modern supermarket form: modern
lamb, lentils, barley, Greek yoghurt, olive oil, figs, fish. Modern agriculture
subtly alters micronutrient values and some dairy is fortified. That is fine.
The question is simply: **can you eat very well today from their pantry?**

**B · Nutritional historical reconstruction.** Strip out fortification, estimate
ancient cultivars, historical mineral content, period processing. A much harder
archaeological problem, and not the one being asked.

---

## Three comparators, not two

"Modern diet" is a useless comparator — someone eating salmon, lentils, oats,
yoghurt, almonds and olive oil is also eating a modern diet, and probably beats
our Hittite noble. So:

| | |
|---|---|
| 🥇 **Optimised modern whole-food diet** | Deliberately designed, no historical restriction |
| 🥈 **Affluent Bronze Age pantry** | Supermarket foods, historically available only |
| 🥉 **Typical contemporary US pattern** | Actual intake, refined and ultra-processed included |

The expected finding — that the Bronze Age pantry lands surprisingly close to
first and clearly ahead of third — is far more defensible and more interesting
than "people used to eat better."

## The "minor noble" model

Not about recreating an aristocrat's meals. It sets the **assumed level of food
diversity**, and it is justified because the surviving recipe corpus is itself
disproportionately palace, temple and affluent cooking.

**Grains** barley · emmer/farro · einkorn · bread · groats and porridges
**Pulses** lentils · peas · chickpeas · broad beans
**Vegetables** onion · garlic · leek · leafy greens · cucumber and gourds · herbs
**Fruit** figs · dates · grapes · raisins · pomegranate · apples and pears
**Fats** olive oil · sesame oil · animal fats · dairy fats
**Animal** lamb and mutton · goat · beef · pork where appropriate · poultry · eggs · fish · shellfish
**Dairy** milk · fermented milk · cheeses and curds
**Extras** nuts · sesame · flax · honey · beer · wine

That is not an impoverished diet. It is a very broad whole-food pantry.

**It also solves the "they ate enormous amounts of grain" objection**, which is
largely a wealth question — cereal is the cheapest scalable calorie. An affluent
household eats lentil-and-barley stew *plus* roast lamb, garlicky greens,
sheep's cheese, figs and walnuts, an emmer loaf, olive oil and wine. That is a
completely different nutritional proposition from 2,600 kcal of barley ration.

## Scorecard — Bronze Age pantry vs typical contemporary US pattern

| | Bronze Age pantry | Typical US |
|---|---|---|
| Whole grains | **Very high** | Low–moderate |
| Legumes | **High** | Low |
| Vegetables and herbs | **High if deliberately varied** | Often inadequate |
| Whole fruit | **High** | Often inadequate |
| Fibre | **Very high** | Low |
| Added refined sugar | **Essentially none** | High |
| Refined starch | **Low** | High |
| Ultra-processed food | **Essentially none** | Very high |
| Fermented foods | **Common** | Variable |
| Nuts and seeds | **Common** | Often low |
| Protein | Plenty | Plenty |
| Fish | Potentially substantial | Variable |
| Micronutrient diversity | **Potentially excellent** | Highly variable |
| Sodium | **Entirely under our control** | Often very high |
| Calories | Easy to over- or under-do | Usually easy to over-do |

For scale: US adults get roughly **53% of calories from ultra-processed foods**;
**83%** fall below recommendations for beans, peas and lentils; about **22%**
meet total vegetable intake. There is no Bronze Age equivalent of any of it.

This reads less like a primitive diet and more like a recognisable
Mediterranean whole-food pattern with an unusual grain profile and no New World
crops. Current US dietary guidance pushes toward whole, nutrient-dense foods
and away from refined carbohydrate and added sugar — the historical constraint
forces you in the same direction more or less by accident.

## What stops being a problem under the affluent-pantry assumption

- **Protein** — downgrade from concern to non-issue. Lentils, peas, grain,
  dairy, sesame, nuts, fish and meat between them make it hard to design a
  varied, calorie-adequate menu that is protein-deficient. Complementarity does
  not need to happen at every meal; the daily pattern is what matters. Barley
  bread + lentils + yoghurt is already doing the work before any lamb arrives.
- **Calcium** — probably excellent with regular fermented dairy and cheese;
  sesame and greens add more.
- **B12** — no meaningful concern with dairy, meat, fish and occasional eggs.
- **Vitamin C** — much less of a problem than the "brown bread, meat and dried
  dates" caricature suggests, given fresh greens, herbs, alliums, fruit and
  pomegranate.

---

## The remaining real vulnerabilities

1. **Iodine.** The interesting one. Maldon is 100% sea salt flakes with no
   iodide — non-iodised sea salt is **under 0.8 μg per ¼ tsp** against roughly
   **70–78 μg** for iodised. "Sea salt" sounds like it should carry iodine and
   does not. **Calculate the diet without iodised salt first:** if regular fish,
   dairy and eggs already reach ~150 μg/day there is no problem. That is
   something to discover rather than assume. If there is a gap, the fix is
   trivial and worth stating plainly — Maldon for finishing and character, a
   little iodised salt for routine cooking.
2. **Vitamin D.** A genuine weakness, though modern fortified dairy complicates
   the experiment.
3. **Omega-3 EPA/DHA.** A menu-design problem more than a pantry problem. A
   coastal Aegean or Egyptian reconstruction with periodic fish does well; an
   inland Hittite one leaning on sheep does not. Flax is worth investigating.
4. **Iron and zinc bioavailability.** See below — calculate alongside nominal
   intake, never instead of it.
5. **Saturated fat.** Could get excessive if elite dishes heavy in mutton,
   butter and cheese dominate the reconstruction.
6. **Alcohol.** Could be surprisingly significant at historical beer and wine
   frequency. Score it separately from food, and model both the reconstruction
   with beer and the same diet with water substituted.
7. Possibly **vitamin E and fatty-acid balance**, depending on which oils, seeds
   and nuts are allowed.

That is a short and manageable list of weaknesses.

## The twist worth building a feature on: phytate

A nutrient table saying *14 mg iron* implies a day's worth. It is not. Whole
cereals and pulses carry **phytate**, which binds iron and zinc and can
substantially reduce absorption — and the effect is largest in exactly this kind
of grain-and-pulse-heavy, occasional-meat diet.

The historically interesting part: **soaking, sprouting, long fermentation and
sourdough all reduce phytate and improve mineral bioavailability.** The
techniques are not merely culinary. A long-fermented emmer loaf is not
nutritionally identical to "150 g wholemeal flour" in a database, and the
experimental-archaeology side of this project has a real nutritional payoff.

Any iron or zinc figure the site publishes needs this caveat attached. Regular
meat also helps considerably.

---

## Corpus diet vs staple diet

Written recipes over-represent elites, because elites generate records.
Averaging the surviving corpus produces a Bronze Age diet that is **too rich**.
Model both — it maps directly onto the commoner/elite dial in `accents.js`:

- **Corpus diet** — the surviving and reconstructed recipes. Likely higher in
  saturated fat, meat, alcohol and calories.
- **Staple diet** — the everyday system implied by grain rations, pulses,
  household agriculture and occasional animal products. Very high in
  carbohydrate, fibre and phytate; more marginal on available iron and zinc.

---

## The thesis

> Take away scarcity, contamination and inequality, and the Late Bronze Age
> pantry is not nutritionally primitive at all. Someone with the food access of
> a modestly affluent Bronze Age household, shopping in a modern supermarket,
> can build a diet remarkably close to what modern nutrition considers a
> high-quality whole-food pattern.

Then it becomes empirical: **how close?**

---

## First concrete test

Build a **2,200-kcal representative "minor noble" day** using only the site's
ingredient corpus, and compare it nutrient-for-nutrient against a typical US day
and against modern recommended targets. That single test tells us quickly
whether the intuition holds.

Longer term: a **seven-day menu** run against modern requirements. A week is a
defensible unit; one dish is not.

Per recipe, beyond an ordinary panel: what Bronze Age eating does better here ·
what modern eating does better · likely nutrient gaps · bioavailability caveats
· historical vs modern portion · how this dish contributes to a complete day.

**Framing rule throughout:** never *"a Hittite consumed 780 calories for lunch."*
Always *"nutritional analysis of our reconstruction."*
