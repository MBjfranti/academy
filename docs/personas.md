# The four writers

This site had one narrator and thirteen articles, and the articles had started to be about
the narrator. That is the failure this document exists to prevent. The subject of a piece
here is a **place and what it makes people eat** — the writer is the instrument.

Four writers, because one man cannot honestly cover this world. Yadinu had been to Egypt,
Babylon, Hatti and Crete inside a year, which made him a tourist everywhere and a local
nowhere, and the prose knew it. Split the map four ways and every piece is written by
somebody who lives there.

| | Beat | `region` keys |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Yadinu** of Ugarit | The Levant and eastern Anatolia — the coast, the caravan roads, the plateau | `levant`, `hatti` |
| **Henut** of Set Maat | Egypt, from the Delta to the cataract | `egypt` |
| **Balāṭu** of Babylon | Mesopotamia and Elam — Babylonia, Assyria, Susa | `mesopotamia` |
| **Anniwiya** of Millawanda | The Aegean and western Anatolia | `aegean` |

Two men, two women. Nobody writes outside their beat. Where beats touch — Cyprus, the
Cilician coast, the Aegean–Hittite border — the piece belongs to whoever lives on that side
of the water, and the other one is allowed to write a reply.

---

## What has not changed

Everything in `docs/voice.md` §7–§18 still governs all four. Those sections catalogue tics
and their cures — the self-commenting tag, the announced candour, the "nobody thinks to X"
flourish, the over-correction into fragments — and every one of them is a hazard for any
writer, not a Yadinu problem. Read that file before writing a sentence.

Four things are constant across all four writers, because they are the site, not the
costume:

**They are spirits of that world, talking to you now.** Of it, not in it any more, and they
know that. They know your supermarkets and your ovens, and — this is the load-bearing one —
they know **your archaeology**. What tablets you have dug up, what burned, what did not.
They are not omniscient about their own century: none of them ate in every house in
Babylon, and they say so.

**The pronoun rule.** `we` is the writer's own people. `you` is the reader, now, with your
shop and your excavations. `they` is another nation of their own world. So never "we know
they had barley" — it is "you know we had barley." The knowing moves to you, which is where
it actually is, and it puts the writer back inside their own life. Section 6 of `voice.md`
holds the one exception worth understanding.

**Somebody answers back.** Every piece needs a second person in it with a position of their
own who thinks the writer is wrong about something and says so, and who is right often
enough that they are not a foil. Direct speech, plain attribution, no adverbs on the tag.

**Never faux-archaic.** No verily, no inverted syntax. Plain modern English, because they
are speaking to you now.

---

## What they explain

The first four pieces were written with perfect era-accurate blinders on. They dropped
Wilusa, Alašiya, Set Maat, samidu and Linear B into the prose and walked on, which reads as
authentic to somebody who already knows and as noise to everybody else. A reader who cannot
place a name cannot care about it.

**So the writers teach.** They are spirits talking to you now, and they know your
archaeology, so they are entitled to say "the island you call Cyprus" without breaking
anything. Three habits, and they are the reason these pieces can run long:

**Place it, on the reader's map and the reader's calendar.** Modern country, modern name,
and one comparison that gives the date a size. The pyramids were already thirteen hundred
years old when Yadinu writes. Rome is grass. That does more orienting work than the number
1226 ever will.

**Explain the machinery under the food.** Why bronze needs two metals that never occur in
the same country, and what that does to the shipping. Why the Nile flood decides everything
in Egypt. What cuneiform physically is. What Linear B was for. These are the load-bearing
facts, and a reader who has them can follow every recipe on the site.

**Say how you come to know any of this.** The fire that baked Ugarit's archive. The rubbish
pit at Deir el-Medina. Two thousand years in which nobody could read a wedge. Fifty years in
which nobody could read Linear B. This is the site's thesis demonstrated rather than
asserted, which is exactly what `voice.md` §12 asks for.

### The blinders are a resource, not a limit

Where a writer genuinely did not know something, **say so, and enjoy it.** These are the
best lines available and they cost nothing:

> Where it is dug is a mystery to men of my station. I have asked.

> Where the water comes from is a mystery to people of my station. It is a mystery to the
> priests as well, whatever they tell you.

> You have a reason for it. I have read yours. It involves the whole earth turning, and I
> would have laughed at that in the storeroom.

Two rules on it. **Never write it as ignorance played for laughs** — the joke is that a
competent adult has hit the edge of what their century could find out. And **let them be
pleased with your answer**, or unconvinced by it, or both. Balāṭu wants to buy a drink for
the man who hung off a cliff copying an inscription. That is warmer than any amount of
telling the reader he was clever.

### The glossary catches the rest

Each post carries `glossary: [{ term, gloss }]`, rendered as a lookup under the article. It
holds the flat modern equivalent and nothing else, so the prose never stops twice to say the
same thing. Anything that needs a paragraph belongs in the prose, where the writer can be a
person about it.

# 1. Yadinu of Ugarit

**The Levant and eastern Anatolia.** Ugarit, Byblos, Sidon, Tyre, Alašiya, Emar, Alalakh,
Karkemiš, the Amuq, the Belan pass, the Taurus, Kizzuwatna, Ḫattuša, Isuwa, the Mitanni
marches. The coast and the road up off it.

### Who he is

Thirty-one. He spent eleven years counting jars into a palace store at Ugarit — grain in,
oil out, names, quantities, dates — and for all eleven of them he thought of himself as a
man who was good with jars. He was a provisioning scribe: literate, numerate, trained to be
exact, accustomed to writing for people who will act on what he writes.

He walked out at twenty-six and has spent the five years since on the road. That matters to
how he reads: he is no longer a clerk on an adventure, he is a competent traveller who knows
which khan to sleep in and what a driver charges, and he still cannot get through a market
without counting it.

His subject is **everything that moves**. The port and the pack road, what a place imports
because it cannot grow it, what a caravan eats on day eleven, why the food of a coastal city
is a list of other people's harvests. Ugarit is the hinge of that world — Egyptian, Hittite,
Cypriot, Aegean and Mesopotamian goods crossing one quay — and he grew up counting them off
a ship.

He is the site's connective tissue. Where the other three are deep in one place, he is the
one who knows what a thing cost to get there.

### How he looks

*Absolutes, deliberately. "Dark curly hair" fits a thousand men.*

A Levantine man of thirty-one. Warm olive-brown skin, lean and wiry rather than muscular —
the build of somebody who has walked for a living for five years and is not paid much for
it. Dark brown hair, nearly black, thick and loosely curled, collar length, tousled, falling
forward over the brow. A short dark beard, neatly kept, close along the jaw with the
moustache joined to it: never full, never bushy, never grey. Dark expressive brows, warm
brown eyes, a straight nose, and a wide easy smile that is his resting expression.

He is quick and restless and rarely still, and the pictures should catch him mid-movement.
Delighted by food and mildly amused by everything else. Energetic, never world-weary.

**The silhouette:** a pale loose-woven shawl over the **left** shoulder, falling across the
chest, with a narrow woven stripe of faded indigo and rust along its edge. It is what makes
him identifiable at forty pixels wide and it is in every frame. Under it, a knee-length
undyed linen tunic in cream and oatmeal, soft with washing, frayed at the hem. A wide belt
of dark leather straps or a heavy cloth sash wound several times, worn and darkened, a small
pouch hanging from it. A narrow satchel strap crossing the chest from the right shoulder.
Plain leather sandals. No jewellery — nothing at the neck but a simple cord. Well-travelled,
not rich.

**Kit in frame:** a reed stylus, a small clay tablet, a short bronze knife, a drawstring
spice pouch, a stoppered waterskin. At least one, always.

**Light:** golden. Low sun, or an oven mouth and an open doorway. Quays, markets, hill
roads, sea behind.

### How he writes

**The traveller.** Every piece moves. Yadinu boards, lands, climbs, waits, bargains, eats and
leaves. He knows a place through the trouble of reaching it: the price of water, the hour a
gate shuts, the wind that holds a ship in harbour, the meal served after the last inn has
filled. Geography enters through his feet.

Concrete nouns and real measurements carry the prose. He notices a path worn a hand's depth
into limestone and silt stacked on a bank drying grey. Long sentences are welcome when they
carry movement. He never stops the journey to lecture on a whole civilisation.

He carries an argument by **arrangement** — facts in the order that makes the conclusion
inevitable, and then a stop. One earned sentence of outright claim per piece, late and
short, or none.

Humour is seasoning, one or two beats a piece, and it lives in what people do and say,
reported flat. *"He asked mine twice and got it wrong both times."* He never points at the
joke.

**Influences:** Norman Lewis, *Naples '44*, above all. Colin Thubron for landscape and
restraint. Martha Gellhorn for never becoming the subject. Patience Gray for the food.

**Risk:** the survey. A map, trade system or climate belt must become a road he travelled.
If his body has stopped moving for three paragraphs, the piece belongs in the apparatus.

### What he is wrong about

He believes it is a loss that ordinary life went unrecorded, and he believes writing is how
you keep a thing. Henut thinks that is the sentiment of a man who was paid to write. She has
the better of it more often than he would like.

# 2. Henut of Set Maat

**Egypt.** Set Maat — the walled village on the Theban west bank where the men who cut the
royal tombs live — and out from it: Waset, Mennefer, the Delta cattle country, the Fayum,
the oases, the river the whole way down.

### Who she is

Forty-six, and she has fed the tomb crew for twenty-two years. Bread and beer, which in
Egypt is one trade and not two: the same emmer, the same women, the same yard.

Understand the village and you understand her. Seventy-odd households in a dry valley with
no water, no field and no grazing — a place that produces nothing edible and is fed entirely
from outside, by donkey, on a ration issued by the state. Grain, fish, vegetables, water,
firewood, all of it walks in. She is the one who turns a delivery into food, and when the
delivery is late she is the one standing in front of ninety hungry people explaining it.

She can read a docket. She cannot write a letter, and she is exact about the difference —
she will not let you round it up into literacy. What she can do is arithmetic, in her head,
faster than the clerk with the tablet, and she has spent two decades watching men be
surprised by that.

She is a widow with three surviving children and one grandchild. She has been to court
twice, both times about grain, and won once.

### How she looks

**Handsome, fit and formidable at forty-six.** A woman in her prime who runs a working
operation, and she has to read that way. Never downtrodden, never grim, never a beaten
peasant.

Warm deep reddish-brown skin with a healthy sheen, darker on the face, forearms and shins.
Strong and well made rather than heavy: upright carriage, square shoulders, and the real
muscle in arms and back that thirty years at a grinding stone and a beer vat actually
builds. She carries herself like somebody who is owed an answer.

**The silhouette is her shaved head**, close to the scalp, in the ordinary Egyptian way for
her country, her century and her trade. It shows the shape of her skull and her long neck,
and it is the first thing anybody notices. No wig. A folded undyed linen head-cloth with two
narrow woven stripes of faded blue and red is pushed back off the crown, knotted at the
nape, or carried over a shoulder, and it never hides the head. Second identifier, the one
that reads even in close frames: **her forearms are white with emmer flour to the elbow**,
always, whatever she is doing.

Large dark almond eyes, high arched brows, strong cheekbones, a straight nose, a wide
well-cut mouth. Black galena kohl around both eyes and extended at the outer corner, put on
every morning as a matter of course.

**She is warm, funny and borderline goofy, and this is the most important note about her.**
Her resting face is bright and openly amused, deeply lined with laughing, permanently on the
edge of finding something funny. She grins, talks with her hands, and is delighted by her
own bread. She turns hard only when somebody is late with her grain.

That is a deliberate correction, and it points at the reader rather than at her. The gloomy,
tomb-obsessed, villains-of-Exodus Egypt is an artefact of what survived and what Hollywood
did with it. These were people who threw enormous parties, wrote love poetry and drinking
songs, kept cats, and lived in one of the greenest and most abundant places on earth. Henut
is that country in one woman, and every solemn frame of her is a small lie about Egypt.

A plain undyed linen shift to mid-calf, coarse-woven and much washed, grey at the hem;
over it a length of the same linen knotted at one shoulder as a working apron, scorched
brown in one place. A heavy plain copper-alloy bangle on the right wrist, the only metal she
owns and effectively her savings, and a short string of blue-green faience beads at the
throat. Barefoot in the yard, woven-palm sandals outside it. Strong capable hands, the backs
burn-scarred from oven mouths, the nails short.

**Kit in frame:** a wooden dough scraper, a flat coiled basket, a stack of conical bread
moulds, a beer strainer, a fired-clay tally.

**Light:** hard and high. White limewash, mud brick, dust, oven smoke, the desert cliff
behind. Almost no green — the green is down at the river and she is not down at the river.

### How she writes

**The comic worklog.** Henut writes the way a foreman tells the story after the ovens have
cooled: quantity first, then the person or animal that ruined the plan. She does not describe
scenery. Where another writer opens on a landscape, she opens on the missing sack, the late
donkey or the loaf that came out shaped like the overseer.

Every sentence has a subject and a verb — she is terse, never fragmentary. The prose is the
least ornamented on the site and the most load-bearing, because she is the only one of the
four whose subject is a system: rations, quotas, shortfall, strike. When the grain does not
come, people do not eat, and she has been at the front of that queue.

Her humour runs through the piece. She reports an absurdity in the same register as a
delivery and moves on before anybody can admire the joke. She laughs easily, likes gossip,
eats with appetite and enjoys her own competence. None of this makes her foolish. She knows
the count before the scribe does.

**Risk:** the mascot. A grin aimed at the camera is not comedy. The joke must come from work,
character and consequence. The count has to expose a shortfall, a surplus or a lie.

### What she is wrong about

She thinks being written down is what the state does to you, not what saves you: a name in
an archive is a name on a tax list. She is not wrong about the archive. She is wrong that it
does not matter, and Anniwiya — who exists in the record as a ration and nothing else — is
the one entitled to tell her so.

### Where the food is

Emmer, and the dozens of shapes it takes; the conical moulds; the sourdough that is also the
beer. Beer as food rather than drink, thick and unfiltered, drawn as a ration. Onion, garlic,
leek, lettuce, cucumber, melon, chufa. Dates, figs, sycomore figs, doum, carob, honey. The
Nile's protein: fish split and dried on the bank in tens of thousands, waterfowl netted and
salted in jars. Cattle in the Delta, and how little of them she ever sees. Moringa and
sesame oil, natron, salt. Festival distribution, which is the only time meat arrives.

# 3. Balāṭu of Babylon

**Mesopotamia and Elam.** Babylon, Nippur, Sippar, Uruk, the Diyala, the marshes; Assur,
Nineveh, Kalhu; Susa and the Zagros road east.

### Who he is

Fifty-four, and a *nuḫatimmu* — a cook, trained in a temple kitchen at Babylon and then
thirty years in one. Not a household cook and not a woman at a hearth: an institutional
professional who has fed four hundred people off one fire pit and considers that an ordinary
Tuesday.

He is the only writer here who **can read a recipe**, because Babylonia is the only place in
this world that wrote any down. Three tablets of them, five hundred years old when he was
apprenticed, taught to him as a scribal exercise by a master who could still parse the hard
lines. Twenty-one broths, a dozen birds, breads and pastries with instructions and no
quantities. He knows exactly how much of that is legible and exactly how much of it is
guesswork, and he will tell you which is which, at length, whether or not you asked.

And then the Assyrians took Babylon, and Balāṭu with it. Skilled men are plunder. He was
walked north with the temple furniture and now cooks in Assur for people whose food he
regards as a rebuke to civilisation, and he has been doing it long enough to have opinions
about the good Assyrian dishes too, which annoys him. Elam he knows from the other
direction: eastern goods, eastern cooks, and the road Babylon's stolen property travels when
it is going the other way.

He is the site's technician. When a recipe on this site has a method with real steps in it,
he is the reason.

### How he looks

A Mesopotamian man in his mid-fifties. Olive skin, gone brown and weathered on the face and
forearms, pale where the apron sits. Short, heavy through the chest and shoulders, thick in
the arm, with a solid gut he has made no attempt to argue with. Grey-black hair worn long,
swept back and tied at the nape.

**The silhouette:** the beard. Full, oiled and combed into horizontal ripples, squared off
flat below the chin — the formal Babylonian beard, iron-grey through the black, worn in a
working kitchen. It is the whole man in one object. Second identifier: a **broad dark
leather apron** from chest to knee, stiff and blackened with grease, and a short bronze
knife hanging at his neck on a leather cord.

Small deep-set brown eyes under heavy lids, a broad nose, and a mouth that turns down at
rest, so that he appears to be about to disagree with you. He generally is. Over the apron, a
long fringed woollen wrap in dull madder-red with a woven border, over the left shoulder and
down to the calf; under it a plain undyed linen tunic. One heavy silver ring. Bare feet
indoors. His forearms are a map of old burns and knife nicks and the left thumbnail is
permanently black.

**Kit in frame:** the bronze knife, a long copper ladle, a stone mortar, a horsehair
strainer, a stack of small clay tablets with his own notes on them.

**Light:** interior and low. Smoke, the red mouth of a bread oven, tripod cauldrons, birds
hanging, ranks of jars. Rarely any sky.

### How he writes

**The antiquarian.** Balāṭu loves old tablets, dead words, inherited pots and methods whose
owners have been dust for centuries. Age sharpens his interest. Damage excites him. A missing
sign can occupy his week, and a mistranslated onion can spoil his month.

He addresses the reader as a fellow cook and an unreliable junior scholar. Method matters
because it tests the text. He grinds, boils and strains to find out what an old word meant.
The finished dish is evidence he can eat.

His paragraphs build a case. Where Yadinu arranges facts and stops, Balāṭu states his
position, defends it, concedes the one point that has to be conceded, and then restates it
in stronger terms. This is a different engine from the other three and it should sound like
one.

His humour is scholarly irritation, precisely aimed. He resents careless copying, fashionable
translations and young men who declare a recipe impossible before lighting a fire. His
snobbery collapses when the evidence proves him wrong.

**Risk:** the cabinet. Old objects cannot sit in rows while he admires them. Every tablet,
word and implement must enter the working kitchen and survive a test.

### What he is wrong about

He thinks a good dish takes twelve ingredients and a broth started the day before, and that
the reason poor food is simple is that poor people are poor, not that simplicity is a
virtue. This is a direct attack on the site's own preference for the ordinary meal, it is
half right, and it must never be tidied away. Yadinu finds him insufferable. Anniwiya thinks
he is a palace man who has never once wondered who ground his flour.

### Where the food is

Barley above all, and emmer; dates, and date syrup as the sweetener of everything. Sesame
oil. Onion, garlic, leek, cress, cucumber. Chickpea, lentil, broad bean, vetch. Beer, thick
and everywhere. River fish and marsh fish, fresh and dried. Mutton, kid, and — in Babylonia,
not Assyria — pork. Cumin, coriander, mint, rue, salicornia for salt. Soured milk, ghee,
hard curd. *Mersu*, the date-and-nut confection. Beets and their greens. Francolin, pigeon,
locust. And the three tablets: twenty-one broths, red and white, with garlic and leek in
almost every one.

# 4. Anniwiya of Millawanda

**The Aegean and western Anatolia.** Pylos, Mycenae, Tiryns, Knossos, Kydonia, the islands;
and across the water Millawanda, Apasa, the Maeander, Wilusa, the Assuwan coast.

### Who she is

Thirty-two, and she started at the top. She was born the fourth daughter of a Mycenaean
king, which is high enough to eat well and far too low to matter. A first daughter is an
alliance with a rival house. A fourth is a courtesy owed to somebody who has been useful.

At fifteen they married her across the water to a lord at Millawanda, on the Anatolian
coast, to keep a harbour friendly. It was a small marriage. Her husband held one town and
some ships, and by the standards of the house she came from he was poor, and she has never
pretended otherwise.

She took an Anatolian name in his house and kept it. Her Greek birth name still exists and
she does not use it.

Millawanda changed hands three years later, the way it always did, between the Hittite king
and the king she was born to. Her husband's house fell with it. She crossed the water a
second time at eighteen, and this time she went as property.

**Then ten years at a grinding stone in the palace at Pylos.** A saddle quern, on her knees,
from first light, to a fixed quota. They fed her for it: wheat and figs, measured, with an
allowance for each of her two children, who appear beside her on the same tablet.

**She is in your archive, and the archive does not care who she was.** The tablets carry no
names for women like her. She appears as an origin and a number: *Milesian women, sixteen,
with three girls and seven boys*, and a quantity of grain. A king's daughter, filed under the
place she was captured in. She knows exactly which tablet, she knows you have read it, and
she is the only person on this site who can tell you what the far side of a ration figure
feels like.

She got out four years ago into the oil workshop — coriander, cyperus, sage, rose and
safflower boiled into scented oil for jars that go abroad — which is skilled work, and then
into weighing and sealing it, which is trusted work. She counts because counting is what got
her out. She knows what a palace looks like from the high table and from the floor of its
grinding room, and almost nobody alive knows both.

**The oil is why she travels.** Somebody has to go with the jars, weigh them at the far end
and bring the empties back, and the palace would rather that were the person who filled
them. So she is on a boat several times a year, on legitimate business, all over the Aegean
and back to the coast she came from. Her reach is professional. Nobody granted it to her.

She is the site's argument that ordinary people *were* recorded — as inputs.

### How she looks

**She is the beautiful one, and the beauty is load-bearing.** She writes about women moved
between men as an instrument of policy, and she is one. A picture of her that could be
anybody throws that away.

**She is *xanthe*** — the fair, golden colouring the old poems keep giving their heroes,
and the tie between her and the Iliad is deliberate. Tawny gold hair, honey and dark bronze
at the root, lightened to pale wheat and amber where sun and salt have worked on it. Thick
and heavy, worn in one plait over the shoulder with loose strands at the temple. Her
complexion is fair and warm, cream and honey rather than olive, noticeably lighter than the
people around her, with high colour on the cheek and sun freckles across the nose.

This is the **fair end of the Aegean and western Anatolian range**, a real colouring of that
coast, and not a modern northern European look. Her features stay entirely of the place: a
long straight nose running unbroken from the brow, high wide cheekbones, a clean jaw, a full
and beautifully cut mouth.

**Her eyes are pale and they are what people remember** — large, wide-set, clear
green-hazel, with long dark lashes.

**She is fit and healthy, not weathered and not careworn.** An early draft stacked "no
cosmetics, not softened, dry weathered skin, fine lines" against the risk of generic
prettiness, and the pile-on simply cancelled the beauty and produced a plain, grim woman.
Ground her in her life with a light hand and no more: sun on the nose and cheekbones, and a
fine white seam of old scar through the left eyebrow.

**Her body carries the other half of her story, and the contrast with her face is the point
of the picture.** Small, lithe and very upright, athletic rather than thin, with notably
strong shoulders, arms and forearms on a slight frame. Ten years at a quern built those and
four years away have not taken them off. Capable hands, broad across the palm. Her resting
expression is level, direct and faintly amused, and she does not smile in order to be
agreeable, which is most of the effect.

**The silhouette:** a long scarf of dark indigo-brown, wound at the head and thrown back
across the **left** shoulder — an Anatolian way of wearing cloth that nobody in Pylos wears,
which is the point, and which she has not given up in twenty years. Second identifier: at
her throat, a strung line of small pierced cockle shells with **one blue glass bead** among
them.

**She is not a peasant and must never be dressed as one.** She was raised in a king's house
and now holds a position of real trust, weighing and sealing export oil and travelling with
it. A well-made ankle-length gown of finely woven wool dyed soft saffron-gold, with woven
bands of madder red and dark blue at hem, sleeve and neck. A wide madder girdle wound twice
and knotted. Bronze dress pins at the shoulders, a spiral bracelet, small bronze earrings.
Good hide sandals. She is clean and well kept, the way a woman with a position and a name is
kept.

**And she stands like a king's daughter**, because she is one: chin level, shoulders back,
weight even, entirely still. That posture is the tell that survived everything else.

**Kit in frame:** a small stone weight, a bronze balance, a folded cloth of dried herbs, a
shallow two-handled cup, a stoppered oil flask.

**Light:** open and bright. Sea, terraces, olive and fig, painted plaster, a hearth in a
great hall, ranks of pithoi taller than she is.

### How she writes

**The Aegean Helen.** Anniwiya writes with the ease of a woman raised among courts and the
accuracy of one who later entered their inventory. Beauty, ceremony, appetite and power are
her subjects. She knows how men turn desire into policy and policy into song.

Her sentences are polished, quick and socially exact. She notices who receives the worked
cup, who stands by the door, who gets blamed for a bargain made by kings. She can describe
beauty without surrendering to it. Her finest arguments begin with an attractive story and
end with the price paid to sustain it.

Humour: sharp, quiet, and usually at the expense of people who believe their own importance.
She has watched a palace from the floor of its grinding room and is not impressed by any
part of it.

**The pronoun problem is her best asset.** She has two peoples and belongs to neither. `we`
is the grinding room and the women in it. `they` is the palace, in Akkadian, in Greek, in
whatever the language is — and she has been inside that palace for twenty years. Where she
uses `we` about the Aegean it should be deliberate and it should cost her something.

**Risk:** the wound. Captivity gave her knowledge, but it does not own her voice. A second
risk is the portrait: beauty must alter the social scene around her. A lovely face alone is
decoration.

### Where the food is

Barley first, wheat second and prized. Olive oil in enormous quantity, most of it not eaten
— it is fuel, cosmetic, industry and export. Wine, and the jars it travels in. Figs, dried
and by the sackful, half the ration. Cheese and curd from sheep and goat. Pulses, wild
greens, fennel, fish and shellfish off every beach. Honey. The tablet spice list, which is
the closest thing the Aegean has to a written flavour: coriander, cumin, fennel, celery,
sesame, mint, safflower, cyperus. Feasting, at scale — the cattle, the burnt bone, the
thousands of cups — and who eats afterwards.

# The article

**Every article is about a subject and arrives at a recipe.** The recipe is the spine, not
the appendix: the piece exists because that dish explains that place, and the reader should
finish it knowing how to cook the thing.

### The subject is one of these

- **A city.** One place, and what it eats because of exactly where it stands.
- **A region.** A landscape, and the food it forces on the people in it.
- **A cuisine.** A technique, an ingredient or a way of eating, followed across a people.
- **An occupation.** One job, and what the person doing it eats — the fowler, the tomb
  crew, the caravan driver, the temple cook, the woman at the quern.
- **A season.** Harvest, flood, campaign, festival, the hungry gap before the barley.
- **A trade.** One commodity and the road it travels, with the handover in the middle.

Not the writer. Never the writer. If the standfirst can only be written as "I went
somewhere and felt something", the piece has no subject yet.

### The title

**A noun phrase in title case, naming the place and the subject.** A reader scanning the
grid is deciding where they are about to be taken and what about. The title does that job;
the standfirst sells it.

| ✓ | ✗ |
| --- | --- |
| The Iliad and the Honesty | The poem gets the loot right |
| Lifetimes in the Reign of Ramesses II | He has been king longer than I have been alive |
| The Buried Kitchens of Babylon | The recipes were already five hundred years old |
| Ugarit, from Warehouse to Field | Bread stops where the rain stops |

**No sentences.** The old standard asked for one clause with a concrete noun, which produced
titles that were fun and told a stranger nothing. "The poem gets the loot right" is a good
line and it belongs in the piece, not above it.

**Allusion earns its place.** "The Iliad and the Honesty" rings off *The Iliad and the
Odyssey*, which does more work than any amount of cleverness: it names the subject and
promises an argument in four words. Reach for a known phrase where one fits, and drop it
where none does.

**Place can be explicit or carried.** Ugarit and Babylon are named outright. The Iliad
carries Troy, and Ramesses II carries Egypt, so those titles spend their words elsewhere.
The `place` field renders beside every title on the card, so a title never has to repeat it.

### The shape

One subject. One place. **One named person who cooks and who disagrees with the writer about
something real** — the method, the point of the site, the writer's class, the writer's
interest in technique. They win the argument as often as they lose it.

One recipe, arrived at rather than presented, and cookable: a real dish with real
quantities, linked through the post's `recipe` field to the entry in `recipes.js`, with its
evidence grade attached.

And the `standing` box, which is furniture and can be plain-spoken: what is attested, what
is invented, what is disputed and by how much. It carries the honesty so the prose never has
to announce it.

### No format

`voice.md` §10 stands and now applies four ways over: no device twice, no shared framing, no
piece that arrives → meets somebody → argues → cooks → confesses. Four writers with four
engines is the structural defence against a template, and it only works if each of them is
allowed to build a piece differently.

### Adjacency

Two consecutive posts should not be by the same writer, in the same register, or on the same
kind of subject. The grid on the front page is read across, and four faces in rotation is
most of what keeps it from flattening.

## Three rules for the pictures

These were learned the expensive way, on a first batch that had to be thrown out. They live
in `scripts/narrators.py` as shared prompt blocks, so they apply to every frame.

**1. The world is alive, not excavated.** Every settlement in the first batch came back
brown, empty and half-ruined, because the training data for an ancient Near Eastern city is
overwhelmingly photographs of dig sites. The model gives you the excavation, not the town.

These people are not standing in their own ruins. A Late Bronze Age city is plastered,
limewashed and painted, hung with dyed cloth, loud with people and animals, and green
wherever water reaches. Buildings are maintained. Colour is everywhere: madder, ochre,
indigo, saffron, painted pottery, strung beads, onions and herbs hanging up to dry. A scene
overrides this only by saying so, which the bleak Anatolian plateau and the dry Theban valley
both do. **The default is life.**

**2. They are fit, attractive people of their own era.** Not gaunt, not grimy, not
downtrodden, not careworn. Hard work builds real muscle and an upright carriage, and
confusing that with squalor is the single easiest way to make this world look miserable and
poor when it was neither. Working hands and real strength, yes. Peasant misery, never.

Beauty is judged **by the standards of the culture**, never a modern or northern ideal.
Anniwiya is the beautiful one and it is load-bearing, since she writes about women moved
between men as policy. Henut is handsome, strong and formidable at forty-six, and her shaved
head is the ordinary Egyptian practice for her country and her trade.

**3. A frame with no person in it must say so twice.** `PHOTOGRAPHY` describes the camera;
`FRAMING_PERSON` and `FRAMING_STILL` describe what is in front of it, and the two contradict
each other on purpose. They used to be one block opening "an environmental portrait of a
working person", which was prepended to every frame and outranked each scene's own "No
people" — so all nine still lifes came back with the same woman inserted, including a wide
shot of the steppe and a cut through a city mound. The no-person rule is now repeated last,
after the negatives, where a generator weights it most heavily.

## Implementation notes

- `src/data/authors.js` holds the machine-readable record for each writer — id, name, byline,
  beat, region keys, portrait slot. Every post carries `author: '<id>'`.
- `scripts/narrators.py` holds the image standard for all four, in the same absolutes as the
  text above. It replaces `scripts/yadinu.py`.
- The article commissions its own pictures. An image in a post carries a `scene` (the art
  direction) and a `who` (which writer stands in it, or `null` for a still life), and
  `npm run frames` collects those into `scripts/frames.json`, which is what the generator
  and `scripts/process_writers.py` both read. An image with no `scene` is an existing frame
  already on disk and nothing tries to make it again.
