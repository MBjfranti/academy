## Step 1. The peg and the nut graf

**Why now.** Two things converge. The centenary of Carter's breach of the antechamber
(26 November 1922) and the burial chamber (16 February 1923), and the transfer of the
complete Tutankhamun collection to the Grand Egyptian Museum at Giza, where all 5,000-plus
objects are displayed together for the first time since they were buried. The move forced
every single object out of storage and under a conservator's hands. That is the story: a
century after the most famous excavation in history, the collection is being properly
examined for the first time.

**Nut graf.** *(target: paragraph 6-8)*

> The paradox of Tutankhamun is that the best-known archaeological discovery of the
> twentieth century is also one of its least understood. Carter spent ten years clearing the
> tomb and produced a record that was exemplary by the standards of 1922 and is close to
> useless by the standards of now: objects catalogued, context lost, the unglamorous
> two-thirds of the collection barely described. For ninety years most of it sat in wooden
> crates in Cairo. Only the move to Giza has forced it back into the light, and what is
> emerging is a different boy, a different burial, and a hundred Egyptian workmen who were
> in every photograph and in none of the captions.

## Step 3b. Fact dossier (68 items; ~a third will be used)

Drawn from published record. Anything I am unsure of is marked **[soft]**.

**Numbers and measurements**
1. 5,398 objects recorded in Carter's catalogue.
2. Tomb designated KV62; four chambers; total floor area roughly 110 m².
3. The burial chamber is about 6.4 × 4 m.
4. Carter took ten seasons to clear it, 1922–1932.
5. Steps found 4 November 1922; antechamber breached 26 November; burial chamber 16 February 1923.
6. Four gilded shrines nested around a quartzite sarcophagus.
7. Three nested coffins; innermost solid gold, roughly 110 kg.
8. Gold mask about 10.2 kg.
9. Tutankhamun died at about 19, reigned about nine years.
10. Six dismantled chariots.
11. About 130 walking sticks and staves.
12. Roughly 100 items of clothing and textile **[soft on exact count]**.
13. Two mummified foetuses in the treasury.
14. About 3,500 years between burial and discovery.
15. Harry Burton exposed roughly 1,400 glass plate negatives.
16. Grand Egyptian Museum sits about 2 km from the Giza plateau.
17. GEM cost is in the region of a billion dollars **[soft]**.
18. The complete collection had never been displayed together before GEM.
19. Around 70% of the collection had rarely or never been publicly shown **[soft]**.
20. 145 pieces of jewellery on the body alone **[soft]**.

**Mechanism and conservation**
21. Carter poured molten paraffin wax onto fragile objects to lift them.
22. The resin poured over the mummy had hardened and glued it to the coffin.
23. Carter's team dismembered the mummy to remove it.
24. Organic material in the tomb survived because of the dry, sealed environment.
25. Opening the tomb destabilised that environment permanently.
26. Textiles are stored flat, in inert boxes, in controlled humidity.
27. Leather degrades by collagen breakdown; it shrinks, hardens, and cannot be reversed.
28. Consolidants used in the 1920s are now themselves a conservation problem.
29. Modern practice is minimum intervention and full reversibility.
30. The chariots required a purpose-built lab because of size and fragility.
31. Gilded wood: the gesso layer fails before the gold does.
32. Insect damage in the textiles occurred largely after excavation **[soft]**.
33. Glass plate negatives degrade through emulsion lift and silver mirroring.
34. Cold storage slows organic degradation but risks condensation on transfer.

**People and record**
35. Carter was funded by George Herbert, 5th Earl of Carnarvon.
36. Carnarvon died in Cairo in April 1923 of blood poisoning from an infected mosquito bite.
37. The Times held exclusive rights to the story, which enraged the Egyptian press.
38. The exclusivity deal drove the "curse" coverage in rival papers.
39. Around 100 Egyptian workers were employed at various stages **[soft]**.
40. The Egyptian foremen were called *reis*.
41. Four named reis are recorded in Carter's own papers **[soft]**.
42. The story that a water boy found the first step is widely repeated and poorly evidenced **[soft]**.
43. Burton's photographs almost never caption Egyptian workers.
44. Published prints were frequently cropped tighter than the plates.
45. Egypt's 1922 nominal independence made the find a nationalist symbol immediately.
46. The Antiquities Service ended partage: the collection stayed in Egypt entire.
47. That decision is why the collection is complete and why GEM is possible.

**Deep time and context**
48. Tutankhamun reigned c. 1332–1323 BC, Eighteenth Dynasty.
49. He was probably the son of Akhenaten.
50. He reversed his father's Aten reforms and restored Amun worship.
51. His name was omitted from later king lists, which is partly why the tomb survived.
52. The tomb was robbed at least twice within a decade of burial and resealed.
53. Flood debris sealed the entrance, hiding it under workmen's huts for Ramesside tombs.
54. KV62 is small and irregular for a royal tomb, suggesting it was not made for him.

**Local practice and terms of art**
55. *Qufti*: skilled excavators from Quft in Upper Egypt, a hereditary trade since Petrie.
56. Qurna village sits directly over the Theban necropolis.
57. Qurnawi families were relocated from the necropolis in the 2000s.
58. A *gufa* is a woven basket used to carry spoil.
59. Object numbers in Carter's system are cited as "Carter no." followed by a number.

**Economics and institutions**
60. Tourism is a major share of Egyptian foreign currency earnings **[soft]**.
61. The 2011 revolution and later instability collapsed visitor numbers for years.
62. GEM's opening was postponed repeatedly.
63. The Griffith Institute, Oxford, holds Carter's records and Burton's negatives.
64. Carter's excavation records were published in full online by the Griffith Institute.

**Sensory**
65. KV62 is cut into limestone; the air inside is markedly cooler at dawn.
66. The Valley of the Kings reaches well above 40°C in summer.
67. Conservation labs run cold enough that staff wear fleeces in an Egyptian summer.
68. Gilded surfaces read yellow-green under the lab's colour-corrected light, not gold.

## Step 4. The chain

> Carter's dig was superb for 1922 and unusable now, because he recorded objects and not
> context → so the collection arrived in the twentieth century as 5,398 orphans → and the
> famous gold got the attention while two thirds of it went into crates → so the real
> research frontier is the boring material: linen, seeds, leather, sticks → the GEM move
> forces every object into a conservator's hands for the first time → which is generating
> more new knowledge in five years than the previous ninety → and the same bias that binned
> the linen cropped the Egyptian workmen out of the photographs → so recovering the boring
> objects and recovering the missing names turn out to be the same act of attention.

**Reorder test:** section 4 cannot precede section 2; the linen only matters once you know
the record is object-shaped.
