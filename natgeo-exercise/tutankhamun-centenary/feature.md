# The Boy in the Boxes

### A century after Howard Carter opened the most famous tomb in the world, the objects he took out of it are finally being looked at

> `natgeo-exercise/README.md`. Historical figures and published findings are restricted to
> matters of public record.

---

At twenty to seven in the morning the conservation laboratory is cold enough that Dalia
Mansour works in a fleece, in Giza, in June. She has the magnifying visor pushed up on her
forehead, where it has left a permanent pale band, and she is looking at a piece of linen
about the size of a dinner napkin through a stereo microscope on a swing arm. The linen is
the colour of weak tea. Under magnification it stops being cloth and becomes architecture,
a lattice of individual flax fibres, some of them still holding the twist a spinner put into
them in the fourteenth century BC.

"Listen," she says, and lifts one corner with a spatula the width of a matchstick.

There is a sound, very faint, like a page turning in another room.

"That is what I do not want. When it is too dry it sounds like paper. It should not sound
like anything." She lowers it again and adjusts the humidity readout on the case beside her,
which is holding at 48 percent, and makes a note on a form that will be scanned, filed, and
attached to an object number that has existed since 1923.

The linen is a tunic. It belonged to a boy who died at about nineteen, in roughly 1323 BC,
after a reign of about nine years that ended badly enough that his successors chiselled his
name out of the king lists. He is the most famous Egyptian who ever lived, and his clothes
have been in a box since before Mansour's grandmother was born.

"Eleven months I have been on this one garment," she says. "Eleven months. And it is not a
famous object. Nobody is going to stand in front of it."

She says this without any detectable bitterness, which is somehow worse.

---

The paradox of Tutankhamun is that the best-known archaeological discovery of the twentieth
century is also among its least understood. Howard Carter found the first cut step on 4
November 1922, breached the antechamber on the 26th, and reached the burial chamber the
following February, and it took him ten seasons to empty a tomb whose four rooms together
cover about 110 square metres — roughly the floor plan of a small suburban house. He
produced a record that was exemplary by the standards of 1922 and is close to unusable by
the standards of now. He catalogued 5,398 objects. He recorded where most of them were. He
did not, in any modern sense, record what they were with, what they were on, what had been
spilled on them, or what the arrangement meant, because in 1922 almost nobody thought that
was what an excavation was for.

The result is that Egyptology inherited 5,398 orphans. And because the tomb contained the
only substantially intact royal burial ever found, and because a great deal of it was made
of gold, the orphans were sorted immediately into the ones worth looking at and the ones
worth storing.

For ninety years, most of them were stored.

"The gold gets the money," Mansour says. "The linen gets me."

The Grand Egyptian Museum, which sits about two kilometres from the pyramids and cost
something close to a billion dollars, changed that by accident. Displaying the complete
Tutankhamun collection together for the first time since the objects were buried meant
physically moving all of them, and moving all of them meant taking every single one out of
its crate, assessing its condition, stabilising it, photographing it, and giving it a
barcode. A logistics problem turned into the largest single act of attention the collection
has received since it came out of the ground.

What is emerging from that attention is a different boy, a different burial, and about a
hundred Egyptian workmen who are in nearly every photograph of the excavation and in almost
none of the captions.

---

Salma Abdelrahman, 33, is the registrar responsible for knowing where all of it is, and she
has developed the habit of referring to objects by number before name, which makes her
conversation sound like a series of coordinates.

"620 is the sticks," she says, when asked what she is most tired of. "620 is a group number.
Do you know how many walking sticks and staves there are? About 130. One hundred and thirty
sticks. Some of them have his name on them. Some of them are just sticks, except that
nothing in there is just anything."

Trainee conservator Gehan Fouad, 24, spends her mornings on a bench in the organics lab and
photographs her own hands at the end of every shift.

"My hands are in the picture," she says. "Not my face. My hands. That is the job, and I
wanted a record of it." She has 300-odd of these photographs on her phone. She scrolls
through a few. In most of them the object is out of focus and the fingers are sharp, which
is the opposite of how the collection has been photographed for a hundred years.

---

The collection is entire, and that is not an accident either. In 1922 Egypt had just been
granted a heavily qualified independence, and the discovery landed into that politics like a
brick through a window. Under the arrangement that had governed excavation for decades,
foreign expeditions expected *partage*: a division of finds, with a share going abroad. The
Antiquities Service refused it here. Everything stayed.

That refusal, made in a bad temper in the middle of a diplomatic row, is why a visitor in
Giza today can see a complete royal funerary assemblage rather than a good one in Cairo and
a scattering across Europe and North America. It is also why 5,398 objects had to be stored
somewhere, for decades, on a budget, in a city that had other problems.

Rifaat Sobhy, 71, was a storeman at the Egyptian Museum in Cairo for thirty-four years. He
declined to be interviewed twice. On the third approach he talked for two hours without
being asked a second question.

"You want me to say the crates were badly kept," he says. "That is what people come for now.
The crates were kept. Do you know what that took? Do you know what it is to move a wooden
box in July with no lift and be told the box is priceless and also that there is no money
for a trolley?"

He describes a store where the temperature swung twenty degrees between night and afternoon,
where the wood of the crates was itself a hazard because wood off-gasses acids that attack
textile and metal, and where the great enemy was not theft or fire but the slow, invisible
chemistry of a bad container.

"Nobody came," he says. "For years, nobody came. Then everybody came at once, and now it is
my fault."

Hassan el-Deeb, 61, the chief conservator for organic materials, is more forgiving than his
own field usually is about the past. He has a ridge in his left thumbnail from forty years
of holding a scalpel the same way.

"In 1972 we had one refrigerator for the whole collection," he says. "One. For a boy king."

He is currently fighting the material he says is the worst in the building, which is not the
gold, or the resin, or the gilded wood whose gesso layer fails long before the gold does. It
is leather.

"Leather remembers everything that has ever happened to it and then it tells you all at
once," he says. Collagen, the protein that gives hide its structure, breaks down
irreversibly under heat and humidity cycling. The leather shrinks, hardens, curls, and
cannot be relaxed again without destroying it. Six dismantled chariots came out of that
tomb, complete with harness. The wood is difficult. The gilding is difficult. The leather is
a negotiation with something that has already decided.

---

The interesting material, almost everyone in the building agrees, is the material nobody
photographed in 1923.

Noha Saad, 29, works on botanical remains and keeps her samples in cigarette tins, which she
is aware looks unserious and which she says are inert, stackable, and free.

"Sixty-two species," she says. "Garlic. Coriander. Watermelon. Chickpeas. Almonds. Doum
fruit. Somebody stood in a room and chose these, and then somebody else packed them, and
they are still here, and nobody has read them."

Her point is not sentimental, and she gets slightly impatient explaining it. A royal burial
was a provisioned journey. The food in the tomb was not symbolic garnish; it was inventory,
and inventory is data. What was in season tells you when the burial happened. What was
imported tells you what the trade routes were carrying in the 1320s BC. What was chosen over
the equally available alternative tells you something about ritual, or taste, or the panic of
a burial assembled in a hurry, which this one clearly was.

The tomb itself is small, irregular, and laid out unlike any other royal tomb in the valley.
The strong suspicion is that it was never meant for a king, and that when a nineteen-year-old
died unexpectedly somebody's private tomb was requisitioned and a state funeral was crammed
into it. Carter's own notes describe objects jammed into the antechamber at angles, chariots
sawn apart to get them through the door, and a burial chamber so tight that the outermost
gilded shrine stood a few centimetres from the walls.

"They gave me a shelf," Saad says. "I asked for a room."

Mansour's textiles carry the same kind of unread information. There are roughly a hundred
items of clothing in the collection: tunics, shawls, loincloths, gloves, socks that were
made to be worn with sandals. Some are elaborately woven with gold thread. Many are plain,
domestic, and worn — worn in the literal sense, showing wear at the elbow and the neck, on
garments that had been used before they were packed.

"That is the thing that stopped me, the first year," Mansour says. "Not the gold ones. The
ones with a mend in them. Somebody mended the king's clothes, and then somebody put them in
his tomb anyway, still mended."

She has been building a technical record of weave density, spin direction, and dye
chemistry, one garment at a time, at a rate of roughly one a year. At that rate the textile
collection will be described in about the year 2100.

---

Which brings the argument to the photographs, and to the fight the building is currently
having with itself.

Harry Burton, an English photographer lent to Carter by the Metropolitan Museum of Art,
exposed roughly 1,400 glass plate negatives at the tomb between 1922 and 1932. They are the
reason the excavation is visually famous. They are also, in a working sense, the excavation's
primary record, because they capture what Carter's notes do not.

Colin Ferris, 55, looks after them in Oxford.

"Fourteen hundred and some plates," he says. "Glass. Each one is a window pane with a
photograph on it, and each one weighs roughly what you think it weighs." Glass negatives fail
in specific ways: the emulsion lifts from the glass at the edges, and the silver migrates and
throws a metallic blue sheen across the densest areas, called mirroring. Ferris keeps cotton
gloves in his shirt pocket the way other men keep a pen.

He is also the person who first pointed at the crop.

"Look at the print they published," he says. "Then look at the plate. The plate is wider.
There are always more people in the plate."

Wafaa Rizk, 38, an Egyptologist working on matching Burton's plates to object numbers, has
made the consequence of that observation into the loudest argument in the field.

"Count them," she says. "Go and count. There are a hundred and fifty men in these
photographs. Burton names four Europeans and no Egyptians. Not one. That is not an accident
of the period. That is a decision, taken on a specific afternoon, and then taken again, and
again, for ten years."

She carries a printed concordance of plate numbers to object numbers, annotated in three
colours, and she refuses to digitise it on the grounds that she thinks better on paper.
Around a hundred Egyptian workers were employed at the tomb at various stages. The skilled
excavators, the *quftis*, came from Quft in Upper Egypt and belonged to a hereditary trade
that had been supplying every major dig in the country since Petrie trained the first
generation in the 1890s. Their foremen, the *reis*, ran the work.

"I am not saying they were bad men," Rizk says. "I am saying the record has a hole in it
exactly the shape of the people who did the work."

Peter Lindqvist, 67, a visiting historian of archaeology, thinks she is right about the facts
and moving too fast on what they mean. He corrects people mid-sentence and then apologises
for it, sometimes in the same breath.

"Wafaa is right that the names are missing," he says. "I only want us to be careful about
what missing proves. In 1922 nobody captioned anybody. The gardener at Highclere is not
named either. The under-housemaid is not named. This was a culture that did not write down
the names of people who worked with their hands, and it did not write them down in Hampshire
any more than it wrote them down in Thebes."

He accepts the hole. He disputes what kind of hole it is.

"It is a hole, certainly," he says. "Whether it is a wound is a different question, and I
think we have started answering it before we have finished asking it."

Rizk's reply, when this is put to her, is short and delivered without heat. "He can say that
because it is not his great-grandfather."

The magazine cannot settle this, and neither, at the moment, can the field.

---

In Qurna, on the west bank at Luxor, Ahmed Gharib, 52, has a different relationship to the
question, which is that he has never regarded it as open.

Gharib is a foreman on a European mission working a Middle Kingdom site half an hour away.
His great-grandfather worked for Carter. He wears, on a cord around his neck, a brass tape
measure with a cracked leather case, which he says came out of the Valley in 1930 and which
he uses, carefully, most days.

"My great-grandfather is in the photograph," he says. "Second from the left, with the water.
Everyone in Qurna knows this. It is not a discovery for us."

He is politely uninterested in the scholarly argument.

"They argue about whether he was named," he says. "He was not named. We know. We have always
known this. We did not need a conference." He turns the tape measure over. "What I want is
for this to go in the case with the rest of it. That is all. Not an apology. A case."

Qurna sits directly on top of the Theban necropolis, which is why its families have worked
these tombs for two centuries and also why most of them were relocated in the 2000s, a
process nobody in the village describes warmly. Gharib knows things the archive does not.
Asked where Carter's spoil went — the tonnes of chip and debris cleared from the entrance
stair and the corridor, which no modern excavation would ever discard unsieved — he gives
directions to three separate dumps, one of which has a road over it now.

"Nobody wrote it down," he says, "because the people who knew were not the people who wrote."

---

The tomb survived because Tutankhamun was forgotten, and he was forgotten on purpose.

He came to the throne as a child, probably the son of Akhenaten, the king who had shut the
temples of Amun and moved the state religion to a single visible god. Tutankhamun's reign
reversed all of it. The temples reopened. The old priesthood came back. And within a
generation or two, the compilers of the official king lists dealt with the whole Amarna
episode by removing it, which meant removing him.

That erasure is why his tomb was still there. Robbers got into it at least twice within about
a decade of the burial, took what was portable, and were interrupted; the necropolis
officials resealed it both times, and the seal impressions are still on the door. Then, about
two centuries later, workmen cutting a tomb for Ramesses VI dumped their chip down the slope
and built their huts on top of it, and a flash flood sealed the lot under a metre of hard
debris.

For 3,000 years the most complete royal burial in Egypt lay under a builders' rubbish heap,
protected by the fact that nobody had any reason to look for a king who had been officially
deleted.

---

At the Valley of the Kings, KV62 opens at six. Mostafa Nour, 47, has guarded the entrance
for nine years and has strong views about the hour.

"In the morning it is cold in there," he says. "Then the buses come, and it is not."

He means this literally and technically. Every visitor brings body heat, moisture, and carbon
dioxide into a limestone chamber whose atmosphere was stable for three millennia and has not
been stable since November 1922. The wall paintings in the burial chamber carry brown spots
that were present in Burton's plates and have not, as far as anyone can measure, spread since
— which is itself a small, hard-won piece of good news, established only because Burton took
the photograph.

Nour says the tomb smells different after rain, which happens perhaps twice a year. Asked
what it smells like, he thinks about it for a while.

"Like a cellar," he says. "Like a house nobody lives in."

---

Back in Giza, the tunic is going back into its box at the end of the shift. Mansour lowers
it onto a sheet of inert board, supports the folds with rolls of acid-free tissue so that no
crease bears its own weight, and closes the lid. The whole operation takes eleven minutes and
she does not speak during any of them.

The garment is not on display. It will not be on display. It is one of perhaps a hundred
textiles, in a collection of 5,398 objects, from a tomb that was cleared before her
grandmother was born, belonging to a king whose own successors tried to delete him and
succeeded so thoroughly that he outlasted every one of them.

"Next year, maybe the shawl," she says, pushing the visor back up onto her forehead. "It is
in worse condition. It will be quicker, because there is less of it left to be careful
with."

She turns the humidity readout to face the door, so the night guard can see it from the
corridor without opening anything, and goes to find her coat.
