# Photo edit: The Boy in the Boxes

> **Specified, not shot.** Nothing generated. Invented subjects; see `README.md`.

Eleven frames. The controlling idea is that the famous pictures of this tomb are all of
gold, taken in 1922, and every one of them is lit like a jewellery advertisement. This edit
is deliberately the opposite: cold light, working hands, and the two thirds of the
collection nobody photographs. The one gold frame in the set is shot to look like inventory.

---

## 1. OPENER — full-bleed spread

**Assignment.** Dalia Mansour at 06:40 in the GEM organics lab, seen from slightly above and
behind, the tunic laid out on inert board under a swing-arm microscope. She is in a fleece.
The room is grey-blue and empty behind her.

**Technique.** Available light only, pushed hard, no flash anywhere near the textile. Shot
from a stepladder at f/2 so the room falls away and only the linen and her hands hold focus.
The colour temperature is left uncorrected so the lab reads genuinely cold.

**Job.** Establishes that this story happens in a refrigerator rather than a desert, and that
the object at the centre of it is a piece of dull brown cloth. Every reader arrives expecting
gold. This is the correction, and it has to be the first thing they see.

**Caption.** Conservator Dalia Mansour has spent eleven months on this single linen tunic.
The lab runs at 18°C and 48 percent relative humidity, conditions the garment last
experienced inside a sealed tomb.

---

## 2. Full page

**Assignment.** Gehan Fouad's hands, sharp, and the object out of focus behind them.

**Technique.** Macro, 100mm, focus deliberately on the knuckles and the edge of the glove
rather than the artefact. The reverse of standard museum photography, which is the point.

**Job.** Argues the edit's thesis in one image: this collection is made of labour. It also
sets up the Burton crop argument forty paragraphs later without the text having to point
forward.

**Caption.** Trainee conservator Gehan Fouad photographs her own hands at the end of every
shift. She has more than 300 such pictures. In nearly all of them the artefact is out of
focus.

---

## 3. Half page

**Assignment.** The crate store: rows of numbered wooden boxes, one open, tissue spilling.

**Technique.** Long exposure on a tripod in low light, no fill, so the depth of the racking
runs away into black. Include the barcode label on the nearest crate, legibly.

**Job.** The ninety years. A reader needs to see the volume of unopened material to believe
the claim that most of the collection was never examined.

**Caption.** Of 5,398 catalogued objects, the great majority spent most of the past century
in storage. Wooden crates off-gas acids that attack textile and metal; replacing them is a
decade-long programme.

---

## 4. Small inset, runs with the leather passage

**Assignment.** A single piece of chariot harness leather, curled hard, on a neutral ground.

**Technique.** Raking single source almost parallel to the surface, so the shrinkage ridges
throw shadow. Sharp end to end, focus-stacked.

**Job.** Makes irreversibility visible. The text says collagen breakdown cannot be undone;
the picture shows a shape that obviously cannot be flattened.

**Caption.** Six dismantled chariots came from the tomb complete with harness. Leather
degrades by collagen breakdown, hardening and shrinking in ways no treatment reverses.

---

## 5. Full page

**Assignment.** Noha Saad's cigarette tins, open, on a bench: seeds, chaff, a shrivelled doum
fruit, each tin labelled in marker.

**Technique.** Flat overhead copy-stand light, deliberately unglamorous, shot as a grid.
Deadpan.

**Job.** Sixty-two plant species is an abstraction. Forty tins on a bench is not. The
deadpan treatment is the argument: this is data, not treasure.

**Caption.** Botanical remains from the burial include garlic, coriander, watermelon,
chickpeas and almonds — a provisioned journey, and a record of what was in season when a
19-year-old king was buried in a hurry.

---

## 6. Double-page spread — THE HINGE OF THE EDIT

**Assignment.** A Burton glass plate on a lightbox, photographed to include the full plate
edge, beside the published print of the same image, cropped tighter. The extra men are
visible in the plate and absent from the print.

**Technique.** Copy work on a lightbox, plate and print at matched scale, one frame, no
compositing. The honesty of it being a single exposure matters.

**Job.** This is the only frame that can make the archival argument without the reader
taking anyone's word for it. Rizk's claim and Lindqvist's objection both become inspectable.
If one image in this story is remembered, it should be this one.

**Caption.** Harry Burton exposed about 1,400 glass plates at the tomb. Published prints
were routinely cropped tighter than the negatives. Burton captions four Europeans by name
and no Egyptians.

---

## 7. Full page — environmental portrait

**Assignment.** Ahmed Gharib in Qurna at the end of the day, the brass tape measure in his
hand rather than around his neck, the west bank behind him going flat and hazy.

**Technique.** 85mm, single hard low sun from camera left, half the face in shadow, no fill.
He looks at the tape, not the lens.

**Job.** Puts a living face against the archival absence. The text can say a hundred men were
unnamed; only a photograph makes one of their descendants a person.

**Caption.** Ahmed Gharib's great-grandfather worked at the tomb. The brass tape measure came
out of the Valley of the Kings in 1930 and is still in daily use.

---

## 8. Half page

**Assignment.** A detail of the tape measure alone: cracked leather case, worn brass, the
cord.

**Technique.** Specimen lighting, dark ground, shallow but stacked.

**Job.** Gharib's actual request is that this object go in a case. The picture makes the
request legible and lets the reader decide whether it belongs there.

**Caption.** "What I want is for this to go in the case with the rest of it," Gharib says.
"Not an apology. A case."

---

## 9. Small, runs with the deep-time passage

**Assignment.** The resealed door impressions: necropolis seals over an earlier breach.

**Technique.** Raking light, tight, no context. Almost abstract.

**Job.** Carries the fact that robbers got in twice within a decade and officials closed it
again. That is the whole reason the tomb survived and it is hard to state without an image.

**Caption.** Robbers entered within about a decade of burial and were interrupted twice.
Necropolis officials resealed the door both times; their seal impressions survive.

---

## 10. Full-bleed spread

**Assignment.** KV62 at 06:05, empty, Mostafa Nour at the rail, the burial chamber wall
paintings behind. Nobody else in the frame.

**Technique.** Tripod, long exposure, existing tomb lighting only. Shot before opening,
which required arrangement and a season of waiting for the right permission window — the
access is part of the picture.

**Job.** The one moment of straightforward wonder in the edit, and it is placed late on
purpose, after the reader has been taught what they are looking at. Also the only frame that
shows the tomb as a room rather than as a source of objects.

**Caption.** The tomb opens at six. Every visitor adds heat, moisture and carbon dioxide to
a chamber whose atmosphere was stable for three millennia until November 1922.

---

## 11. CLOSER — full page

**Assignment.** The tunic going back into its box: hands, tissue rolls supporting each fold,
lid half on. Mansour's face out of frame.

**Technique.** Same lens and light as frame 1, deliberately, so the edit closes where it
opened. Eleven minutes of work, one frame.

**Job.** Returns to the opening subject, which is the structural requirement, and leaves it
unresolved: the object goes back in a box, and the box is where it stays.

**Caption.** The tunic is not on display and will not be. At the present rate of technical
description, the collection's textiles will be fully recorded around the year 2100.

---

## Rhythm

Spread, page, half, inset, page, **spread**, page, half, small, **spread**, page.

Two full-bleed spreads: frame 6 (the crop) and frame 10 (the tomb). The first is the
argument, the second is the wonder. Deliberately not adjacent, and the argument comes first
so the wonder is informed rather than decorative.

## What the edit refuses

No gold mask. It is the most reproduced object on earth, it would dominate any page it
appeared on, and its absence is the edit's clearest statement about what this story is
actually about. If an editor insists, it runs small, in frame 3's crate store, still boxed.
