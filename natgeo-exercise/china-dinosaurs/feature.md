# The Bottom of a Very Quiet Lake

### China holds the best record anywhere of how dinosaurs became birds, and most of it was dug up by farmers

> 1999 Archaeoraptor affair, the published findings and the institutions are documented
> public record. See `natgeo-exercise/README.md`.

---

Su Meiling has been working the same block of rock since the second week of March, and in
five months she has taken about 40 grams off it.

The block is 31 centimetres across and weighs a little under 9 kilos. It sits clamped in
modelling sand under a binocular microscope in a preparation lab in Fujian province, and Su
works it with a pneumatic air scribe the width of a fountain pen, running at roughly 30,000
strokes a minute, which chatters at a pitch you feel behind your front teeth. She is
listening as much as looking. The tool drops about a semitone when it crosses from mudstone
onto bone, and that note is the only warning she gets.

"Five months on one block," she says, without looking up. "People think that is slow. Five
months is quick, for this."

What is coming out, 40 grams at a time, is the left hind limb of a small theropod dinosaur
that died about 150 million years ago. The femur is 71 millimetres long. The tibia below it
is 134. In nearly every small meat-eating dinosaur anyone has measured, that relationship
runs the other way.

Among living birds a lower leg twice the length of the thigh is a wading proportion, the
geometry of a heron rather than a pigeon. It also turns up in animals built to run on open
ground. Dr Chen Ruoshan, who runs the excavation, will not choose between them, and says the
useful part is what the ratio rules out.

"There is a version of this story where everything is turning into a bird," she says. "The
ground does not show that. It shows a great many animals trying a great many things, and
most of them stopping."

## A very quiet lake

Through the late Jurassic and early Cretaceous a chain of lakes sat across what is now
northeastern and southeastern China, in a landscape that was volcanically busy. Eruptions
dropped fine ash across the water at intervals. The ash sank through a stratified water
column into a bottom layer with almost no oxygen in it, where nothing lived and nothing
scavenged, and settled at a grain size measured in tens of microns.

An animal that died and sank into that was buried whole, fast, in particles fine enough to
take the impression of a single feather barb.

The best known of these deposits, the Jehol Biota of Liaoning and its neighbours, runs from
about 130 to 120 million years ago. Beginning with a metre-long animal described in 1996, its
fossils came out of the ground with the filaments still attached, and a field that had argued
for a century about whether birds descended from dinosaurs largely stopped arguing inside a
decade.

The Fujian beds are older by close to 30 million years, which is why Chen is here. They sit
near the base of the interval that produced birds, in a stretch of record that was until
recently close to blank.

She makes visitors stand still for a moment before she explains any of it.

"You are standing in a lake," she says. "Everything you can see was the bottom of a lake, it
was very quiet down there, and that is the whole reason you came."

## What the quarry looks like

The excavation is 40 minutes from the lab up a track that a van can manage in dry weather,
and it is smaller than almost anyone expects: a stepped cut into a hillside about 18 metres
long and four deep, with a tarpaulin over the working face and a spoil heap below it that is
now larger than the hole.

Nine people work it in season. They take the face down in horizontal spits of about 15
centimetres, and every slab that comes off goes onto a trestle where two crew members split
it with a hammer and a broad chisel and look at both faces.

Most slabs are blank. Chen's tally for the 2023 season records 1,140 slabs split and 31
carrying vertebrate material of any kind, of which nine were worth lifting whole. That is a
hit rate of about one in 127, and she says it is unusually good.

Anything worth lifting gets a number, a photograph in place, a compass bearing, and a
measurement down from a fixed datum peg driven into the top of the cut. The slab is then
jacketed in plaster and hessian, which takes about an hour, and carried down the hill on a
stretcher by four people.

"That hour is the part nobody films," Chen says. "Everybody wants the moment you see the
bone. The hour after it is the part that decides whether it was worth seeing."

The datum peg is the whole difference between this quarry and the ones the rest of this
article is about.

## Paid by the piece

China holds the best record of the origin of birds that exists anywhere, and that record was
assembled, in very large part, by farmers.

The Liaoning beds lie under agricultural land and close to the surface, and they split
cleanly. A person with a hammer and a trained eye can open a slab in a field and find a
complete animal inside it. For about 30 years that is substantially how the specimens have
been found: not by expeditions, but by people who live on top of them and are paid by the
piece.

For most of that period, palaeontologists have bought them. A fossil a scientist declines
does not stay in the ground.

The market that reasoning built has produced some of the most important fossils ever
described, and at least one forgery that fooled a great many professionals, including the
editors of this magazine.

---

It took four months and the agreement of two provincial authorities to get me onto a working
quarry in Liaoning, and when I arrived the man I had come to see spent the first 20 minutes
establishing that I was not from any government.

Wang Jinlong is a farmer and a collector, in that order. He has been turning up fossils on
and around his family's land since he was a boy, and for most of that time nobody had told
him they were worth anything.

"My father ploughed here," he says. "He turned up the stones and threw them in the ditch, and
nobody told us."

He keeps specimens under a bed, in rice sacks, wrapped in newspaper. He showed me four and
declined to show me a fifth, and when I came back to the fifth he changed the subject twice
with a courtesy that made the refusal completely clear.

The four were split slabs. When a fossiliferous bed splits it gives you part and
counterpart: two faces of one break, each carrying half the impression of the same animal.
Laid together they are one fossil. Sold separately they are two objects, and this is the
single most destructive fact about the trade.

"A dealer takes one side," Chen says. "The other side stays in a house, or goes somewhere
else, and now there is half an animal in a museum and half an animal nobody can find, and
neither half will tell you where it came from."

## What it is worth

I asked everybody what a fossil sells for, and for a while I got the answer that this sort of
question always gets, which is that it depends.

It does depend. It also has numbers, and after three weeks I had enough of them from enough
directions to put a chain together.

A farmer who brings a good, complete, palm-sized feathered specimen to a dealer at the
roadside is paid, according to four separate accounts that agreed within about 20 percent,
somewhere between 800 and 3,000 yuan. Call it a month's agricultural income for something
that took an afternoon and a hammer.

The same slab, cleaned and mounted and offered to a collector through a dealer's network,
runs to the low tens of thousands. Mr Tan, who has been doing this since 1997, put a
representative figure at 40,000 yuan and said that the multiple, not the price, is the thing
to write down.

"You are asking the wrong number," he said. "The number is how many times it multiplies
between the field and the wall. It is not two. It is not five."

Exceptional specimens leave that scale entirely. A complete, articulated, unambiguous new
species with feathers preserved is a museum acquisition or a private sale in the hundreds of
thousands, and occasionally past a million yuan, and everybody who quoted me a figure at that
level did so about somebody else's transaction.

Set against that, Chen's institutional budget for acquisitions in 2023 was 260,000 yuan for
the year.

That gap is the reason the argument in the next section exists, and no amount of principle
closes it.

## Buy, or refuse

Chen buys.

"Anton is right," she says, before anybody asks. "Put that in. He is right, and I still buy."

Her case is triage. A specimen refused does not stay safe: it gets split and sold twice,
shipped, glued, or cut down and polished for decorative stone. So she buys, on a procedure
she is faintly embarrassed to call a procedure.

"I have a rule. I pay for the fossil. I pay more for the location. I pay most for the truth
about the location."

In practice that is a premium of two to three times the base price for being walked to the
hole. If the finder takes her to the exact bed and lets her record the horizon, the matrix
and the orientation, the price goes up sharply, because that information is worth more than
the bone. Without it a fossil can be described anatomically and support almost nothing else:
no reliable date, no environment, no association with whatever it was buried alongside.

She has paid the location premium 14 times in nine years and been walked to a hole she
believed was the right one on 11 of them.

Dr Anton Reiss will not buy anything, from anyone.

"Every yuan buys a shovel," he says. "That is the argument in one sentence. The problem is
not the fossil. It is the shovel."

He has paid for the position and keeps the invoice. In 22 years he has turned down three
specimens he believes would have changed his career, can name all three, and does, whenever
anyone suggests the stance is cost-free.

Neither has any contempt for the other, which is rarer than it should be.

"Ruoshan is a serious scientist and I think she is wrong," Reiss says. "She thinks I am wrong.
Neither of us thinks the other is a fool."

I put each position to the other across about a week. Neither moved, neither expected to, and
I am not going to pretend to settle it here.

## The man with the van

Mr Tan declined to speak to me three times.

The first refusal came by message and was polite. The second was in a car park in a county
town and lasted under a minute. The third was a phone call in which he pointed out,
reasonably, that nothing good had ever come to anyone in his trade from talking to a foreign
magazine.

He rang back 11 days later and talked for three hours, at first on condition that none of it
be used, and at the end on condition that some of it was.

He works a territory he described as roughly 400 kilometres across and drives a van he has
twice had modified for carrying stone. He is not a smuggler, he says, in that he does not
personally move material across a border, and he knows some people will not grant him the
distinction.

One thing he wanted said plainly.

"You want me to be the villain," he said. "I moved things nobody else would move. Some of
them are in your museums."

Nobody I put that to denied it. A meaningful share of the material that built this field
passed at some stage through hands doing what Mr Tan does, and the scientists now writing the
provenance rules largely built their careers on specimens collected before those rules
existed.

He also described, flatly, the practice that does the damage.

"A whole animal is one sale," he said. "A whole animal is heavy. Nobody wants to carry it up a
hill."

## Nobody to enforce it

Ms Hao Lifen keeps the regulations on her phone as a photograph, because it is faster than
her department's own system.

She works in provincial heritage administration, where vertebrate fossils are one item on a
list that also includes tombs, temples, kiln sites and two protected bridges. Asked how many
staff she has for fossils specifically, she said one, meaning herself, at about a fifth of
her time.

The rules are not weak. Vertebrate fossils are protected, export is restricted, excavation
requires authorisation. The problem is arithmetic. The fossils lie under private agricultural
activity across tens of thousands of square kilometres, the first person to see any given
specimen is almost always the person who dug it, and by the time anything official knows a
fossil exists it has generally moved at least twice.

"The law works for a fossil somebody reports," she said. "Tell me how many that is."

## The specimen that was not

In November 1999 this magazine published an article describing a fossil from Liaoning as a
missing link between dinosaurs and birds. It was called Archaeoraptor, it had a bird's front
end and a dinosaur's tail, and it had been bought, having already left China, at a mineral
show in Tucson, Arizona.

It was a composite, assembled out of genuine fossil material from at least two different
animals and glued into a single slab that told a story the market wanted to hear.

The magazine retracted it. The affair became the standard cautionary case in the field, and
it is taught to graduate students who were not born when it happened.

The institutional consequence outlasted the embarrassment. A great many journals will now
decline a paper describing a specimen with no documented provenance, which protects the
record and also means some genuine and important fossils, sitting in private hands with
nothing attached, will never be described by anybody.

There is a version of the story in which the episode is humiliating and nothing else. That
version leaves out that one part of the composite turned out to belong to a real animal, and
a significant one, subsequently described properly and now near the centre of what is known
about feathered flight.

Dr Ramesh Iyer runs the scans that would catch it today.

A CT scan reads density through the block. In a genuine slab the internal structure runs
continuously: same matrix, same bedding planes, the same inclusions drifting through at the
same angle end to end. In a composite the bedding stops dead at the join, resumes on the far
side at a different angle, and the adhesive shows as a seam of anomalous density running
through the section like a fault.

"A join is invisible on the surface if the man was good," Iyer says. "It is never invisible
inside. The rock has a grain, the grain has a direction, and glue has no direction at all."

He gave the number twice, as though he wanted it written down.

"Forty minutes on the scanner. Forty minutes, and a great deal of the last 30 years does not
happen."

Dr Peter Yeung directs a museum with two locked cabinets and will discuss one of them.

The first holds about 340 specimens with full documentation: bed, orientation, matrix,
collector, date. He opened it, walked me through four trays, and let me photograph three.

The second holds material that arrived across four decades with nothing attached at all.
Donations, confiscations, a transfer from a provincial collection that closed in 1994, and a
wooden box left at a loading dock in 1998 that nobody has ever claimed.

"I can show you that cabinet," he said, of the first. "I cannot show you the other one, and
you should write that I said so."

Publishing an inventory of undocumented material advertises it to precisely the people he
does not want to hear from, he has no mechanism to establish where most of it came from, and
some of it is almost certainly significant.

"It is not a scandal," he said. "It is a filing problem that was left for 30 years and became
a moral problem by sitting there."

## What colour it was

Some Jehol fossils preserve structures in the feathers that appear to be melanosomes, the
organelles that carry melanin in a living animal. The pigment is long gone. What survives is
the shape of the container, and in living birds the shape tracks the colour: elongate,
sausage-shaped melanosomes go with black and dark grey, spherical ones with reddish-brown.

Dr Marcus Oyelaran maps those shapes across fossil feathers and reconstructs pattern from
them.

"What is left is the container it came in," he says, "and we have thousands of modern birds
to calibrate against."

Professor Ilse Brandt is unpersuaded in a specific way she separates carefully from general
scepticism.

"You have the container and you are inferring the contents," she says. "In some cases I
believe you. In most cases I would like a second line of evidence."

Her objection is about burial itself. Fossilisation involves heat, pressure and time, and
there is experimental work suggesting melanosome geometry can be altered by the process that
preserves it. If a spherical melanosome can be flattened into an elongate one over 125
million years, the correlation the method rests on becomes a hypothesis rather than a ruler.

"I am not the person who says nothing can be known," she says. "I am the person who says this
particular claim is doing more work than its data."

She answers email in three lines, always. I have nine of them.

## After rain

On my last afternoon at the Fujian site it rained for about 40 minutes and then stopped, and
everyone went straight back onto the spoil heap.

A young man everyone calls Little Guo found three specimens last season on ground the trained
team had already walked, and Chen raises this unprompted and with some force.

I asked how he does it. He looked briefly cornered, then said it was the colour.

"After rain, it is a different colour."

I spent the next half hour looking at wet grey shale on which every piece was exactly the
same colour as every other piece, while Little Guo worked along the heap ahead of me,
unhurried, turning over roughly one stone in 30.

He stopped once, crouched, and handed something back without ceremony: a flake the size of a
palm with a dark line across it that I would have called a crack. Chen, arriving, identified
it in under two seconds as the shaft of a feather.

His name will not go on anything. He is on the payroll as seasonal labour at 180 yuan a day,
and when the season closes in November he will look for winter work, and in March, if the
funding holds and the permits clear, somebody will call him.

The block under Su's microscope will not be finished by then. It has perhaps six months left
in it, after which the animal inside will be photographed, scanned, described, argued over and
given a name in Latin, and the name will belong to whoever writes the paper.

Su had already told me how that ends, on the first morning, over the noise of the scribe: 40
grams at a time, and everything she takes off, she takes off once.
