## Step 1. The peg and the nut graf

**Why now.** White sharks have returned to Cape Cod in numbers not seen in living memory,
and they are there because of a conservation success. The Marine Mammal Protection Act of
1972 rebuilt a grey seal population that had been hunted to near-nothing in New England
waters; the sharks followed the seals. In 2018 Massachusetts recorded its first fatal shark
bite since 1936. A coastline whose entire summer economy is people standing in waist-deep
water now has a large predator hunting in that same water, and the towns have to decide what
to do about it in the next budget cycle.

The second peg is a fight over a word. The scientific literature has been moving from
"attack" to "bite" and "negative encounter" for a decade, and the choice is not cosmetic:
what the event is called shapes what gets funded.

**Nut graf.** *(target: paragraph 7-9)*

> The sharks off Cape Cod are the clearest evidence anyone has that the Marine Mammal
> Protection Act worked. That is the difficulty. A statute passed in 1972 to save seals has
> restored a predator-prey relationship older than the human occupation of North America, and
> it has restored it four metres from a beach where children are learning to swim. Nothing
> about that is a failure of management. It is what recovery looks like, and nobody wrote the
> part of the plan that explains it to a town of 3,000 people with a July payroll.

**The unresolved disagreement:** Rele and Kohl, on the word. Rele's case is that "attack"
imports intent, that intent implies malice, that malice funds culls, and that the data show
no shark has ever hunted a person as prey on this coast. Kohl's case is that she was
attacked, that the word describes what happened to her body, and that a discipline
sanitising her experience for its own political convenience is doing to her what it accuses
the tabloids of doing to sharks. **Both are right. The piece does not adjudicate.**

**Local expert outranking the credentialled one:** Tavares reads water from 500 feet faster
and more reliably than the drone software, and Vasquez says so on the record.

**Reluctant source:** Gil Sorensen.

## Step 3b. Fact dossier (74 items; ~a third used)

Marked **[soft]** where I am uncertain and would check before publication.

**The law and the seals**
1. Marine Mammal Protection Act signed 1972.
2. Grey seal, *Halichoerus grypus*; the name translates roughly as "hook-nosed sea pig".
3. Massachusetts paid a bounty on seals into the 1960s **[soft on end date]**.
4. New England grey seals were reduced to a few hundred animals by the mid-20th century **[soft]**.
5. Current US grey seal population estimates run into the tens of thousands **[soft]**.
6. Muskeget Island is a major pupping site.
7. Grey seal pups are born white-coated and moult within about three weeks.
8. Adult male grey seals can exceed 300 kg.
9. Seals haul out on sandbars within metres of swimming beaches.
10. Seal recovery is a documented, intended outcome of federal protection.

**The sharks**
11. White shark, *Carcharodon carcharias*.
12. The lineage is roughly 16 million years old **[soft]**.
13. Mature females can exceed 5 m and 1,000 kg.
14. White sharks are regionally endothermic: a countercurrent heat exchanger, the *rete
    mirabile*, keeps swimming muscle, viscera and brain above ambient water temperature.
15. That endothermy is why they hunt effectively in cold north Atlantic water.
16. Electroreception via the ampullae of Lorenzini detects the electrical fields of muscle
    movement at close range.
17. Countershading: dark dorsal, white ventral, which defeats sight from above and below.
18. Cape Cod white sharks are seasonal, roughly June to October.
19. They hunt seals in water often shallower than 5 m, very close to shore.
20. That shallow-water hunting is the specific local hazard; it is not typical of all
    white shark populations.
21. Tagging uses acoustic transmitters and coastal receiver arrays.
22. A tagged shark is only detected when it passes a receiver, so absence of detection is
    not absence of shark.
23. Satellite tags give position but need the fin at the surface.
24. Individual white sharks are identified by dorsal fin notch patterns, like fingerprints.
25. White sharks are slow to mature and have small litters, so populations recover slowly.
26. They were listed as prohibited species in US Atlantic waters in 1997 **[soft on year]**.

**Risk**
27. Global unprovoked bites run at roughly 60 to 80 a year.
28. Global fatalities run at roughly 5 to 10 a year.
29. Drowning kills orders of magnitude more beachgoers than sharks do.
30. Massachusetts recorded a fatal shark bite in September 2018, the first since 1936.
31. The 1916 New Jersey bites produced the "rogue shark" theory, now discarded.
32. The rogue shark theory drove decades of culling policy.
33. Two competing hypotheses for bites on humans: mistaken identity and exploratory biting.
34. Neither hypothesis is settled; both are actively argued.
35. A white shark releasing after one bite is the common pattern in survivable incidents.
36. Blood loss, not the bite itself, is the usual cause of death.
37. Tourniquet kits on beaches are now standard on Cape Cod **[soft]**.
38. Time to definitive care is the single biggest survival variable on an outer beach.

**Management, and what has been tried**
39. Réunion banned swimming and surfing outside lagoons and supervised areas in 2013.
40. Réunion's bites are principally bull sharks, *Carcharhinus leucas*, and tiger sharks.
41. Bull sharks tolerate fresh water and frequent river mouths and turbid water.
42. Western Australia ran a drum-line programme from 2014; it was abandoned after review.
43. Shark Spotters began in Cape Town in 2004 using elevated human observers and flags.
44. Spotting depends on water clarity, sun angle, and wind; it fails on a grey day.
45. Nets and drum lines kill non-target species including rays, turtles and dolphins.
46. Personal deterrents using electric fields have partial, contested evidence **[soft]**.
47. Drone patrols are increasingly used; detection rates depend heavily on water clarity.
48. Beach closure decisions are made by municipal officials, not scientists.

**Culture**
49. *Jaws* was filmed on Martha's Vineyard in 1974 and released in 1975.
50. Martha's Vineyard is a few miles from the current Cape Cod white shark aggregation.
51. There was no comparable white shark presence there when the film was made.
52. Peter Benchley publicly regretted the film's effect and became a shark conservation
    advocate.
53. The film is widely credited with a lasting collapse in public sympathy for sharks.
54. "Shark attack" as a fixed phrase predates the film but was hardened by it **[soft]**.
55. The International Shark Attack File is maintained at the Florida Museum of Natural
    History.
56. Several researchers and agencies now prefer "bite", "incident" or "negative encounter".
57. Australia's New South Wales agencies moved to "incident" and "interaction" **[soft]**.

**The town**
58. Cape Cod's outer beaches are within the Cape Cod National Seashore.
59. Summer population on the Cape multiplies several times over the winter figure.
60. A single closed beach day is measurable in a snack concession's takings.
61. Purple flags are used for dangerous marine life on many US beaches.
62. Lifeguard staffing is a recurring municipal budget fight.
63. Outer Cape beaches have limited mobile coverage in places **[soft]**.
64. Emergency response to an outer beach can require a vehicle on soft sand.

**Deep time and sensory**
65. The seal-shark predator-prey relationship long predates human presence in North America.
66. Cape Cod itself is a terminal moraine, deposited by the Laurentide ice sheet.
67. The outer beach is actively eroding; it moves metres a year in places.
68. Water off the outer beach in August runs roughly 18 to 20°C **[soft]**.
69. Seal haul-outs are audible from the dunes and smell strongly.
70. A spotter aircraft flies low enough that the beach hears it before it is overhead.
71. White sharks are frequently visible from the air and invisible from the sand at the same
    moment.
72. Grey seals often follow swimmers, which alarms swimmers and is the actual hazard vector.
73. Chumming is prohibited in Massachusetts state waters **[soft]**.
74. Tagging requires the animal alongside the boat, which requires bait, which is itself
    contentious.

## Step 4. The chain

> Congress protected seals in 1972 → the seals came back, in tens of thousands → white
> sharks followed the food to Cape Cod → and unlike most white shark populations these hunt
> in water shallow enough to stand in → which puts a large predator inside the exact volume
> of water a summer economy depends on people standing in → so the risk is real, and tiny,
> and the perception is enormous → which means the manageable variable is human behaviour,
> never the shark → so everything turns on getting people out of the water at the right
> moment and keeping their trust for the next time → and what the event is called determines
> whether the money goes to spotters and tourniquets or to killing sharks → while forty
> miles away, the film that fixed the word in the language was shot in water that had no
> white sharks in it at all.

**Reorder test:** the *Jaws* section cannot precede the mechanism section; the irony only
works once the reader knows the sharks are real and recent.
