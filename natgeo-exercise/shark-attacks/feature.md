# The Word for What Happened

### A law passed in 1972 to save seals has put white sharks back in the water off Cape Cod. Now the towns have to decide what to call it

> `natgeo-exercise/README.md`. The September 2018 Massachusetts fatality is real and appears
> only as a dated matter of public record.

---

Bobby Tavares flies with the window open, which at 500 feet and 90 knots means the cockpit
of his 1974 Cessna is full of a noise you have to shout through and a smell of cold salt
that he says he stopped noticing in about 1996. Wedged into the door frame is a grease
pencil on a lanyard. When he sees something he wants, he marks the glass.

"There," he says, and the pencil goes on the windscreen, a small waxy circle over a patch of
water that looks, from the passenger seat, exactly like all the other water.

It takes about four seconds. Then the patch resolves, the way a word resolves when you hear
it a second time, into a shape roughly the length of a car and the colour of a bruise, moving
north along the outside of the second sandbar at the speed of a jogger.

"Four-metre female," Tavares says. "Maybe four and a bit. She's been up and down this stretch
since Tuesday."

He is 63 and has flown this coastline for 31 summers, first for a fish-spotting outfit
chasing bluefin, now under contract to three towns and a state agency who all want to know
the same thing on the same mornings in July. He banks, comes back south along the beach, and
below the wing there are perhaps forty people in the water, and none of them can see what he
is looking at, and most of them are about 200 metres from it.

"That's the whole job," he says. "That right there. I can see it, they can't, and somebody has
to decide what to do about that before lunch."

---

The sharks are here because of a law.

In 1972 Congress passed the Marine Mammal Protection Act, and among the animals it protected
was the grey seal, *Halichoerus grypus*, a species that Massachusetts had been paying a
bounty on within living memory and had very nearly eliminated from New England. By the middle
of the twentieth century a fisherman could work these waters for years and see a handful.

They are not scarce now. Nobody will give a firm number, because seals move and counting them
is genuinely hard, but the population in US waters runs into the tens of thousands, and on
the sandbars off the outer Cape they lie in ranks a hundred metres long, close enough to the
beach that on a still day you can hear them from the dunes and, depending on the wind, smell
them well before that.

Nora Aiken, 38, has spent eleven summers counting them and has developed the habit of
counting aloud under her breath in ordinary conversation, which she is aware of and cannot
stop.

"*Halichoerus grypus*," she says, watching a haul-out through a spotting scope from the top
of a dune. "Hook-nosed sea pig. Somebody in Latin was having a bad morning."

Then, without a change of tone: "Sixty-one, sixty-two, sixty-four. There's two behind the
big male."

The seals came back because they were protected. The sharks came back because the seals came
back. There is no more complicated mechanism than that, and there is no villain in it
anywhere.

Which is the difficulty. The white sharks off Cape Cod are the single clearest piece of
evidence anyone has that the Marine Mammal Protection Act worked exactly as intended. A
statute passed fifty years ago to stop the killing of seals has restored a predator-prey
relationship considerably older than the human occupation of North America, and it has
restored it about four metres from a beach where children are learning to swim. Nothing about
that is a management failure. It is what recovery looks like. Nobody wrote the part of the
plan that explains it to a town of three thousand people with a July payroll.

In September 2018 Massachusetts recorded a fatal shark bite, its first since 1936.

---

To understand why this coast in particular is a problem, it helps to understand what a white
shark actually is, and the honest answer is that it is a warm animal pretending to be a cold
one.

*Carcharodon carcharias* is a fish, and fish are generally the temperature of the water they
are in. White sharks are not, quite. They carry a countercurrent heat exchanger called the
*rete mirabile*, a dense braid of arteries and veins running alongside the swimming muscle,
in which outgoing warm blood from the muscle passes close enough to incoming cold blood from
the gills to hand over its heat before it can be lost to the water. The effect is a shark
whose swimming muscle, gut and brain run several degrees above the sea around it.

That single piece of plumbing is why there are white sharks in the north Atlantic in the
first place. A cold muscle contracts slowly. A warm one does not. Regional endothermy buys
the animal fast acceleration in water at 18°C, which is roughly what the outer beach runs at
in August, and it buys a digestive system that keeps working on a cold seal.

The sensory equipment is a similar story of specialisation. Along the snout and around the
head are hundreds of small pores, the ampullae of Lorenzini, each one a jelly-filled canal
ending in a receptor sensitive to minute electrical fields. Every contracting muscle in the
sea generates such a field. At close range, in poor visibility, a white shark can locate a
body by its heartbeat.

And the animal is countershaded, dark above and white below, so that from above it disappears
into the seabed and from below it disappears into the surface glare. Erin Vasquez, 47, a
state fisheries biologist who has been tagging these animals for a decade, says the
countershading is the part that non-specialists underrate.

"People ask me how big it has to be before you'd see it," she says. "That is the wrong
question. I have been three metres from a four-and-a-half-metre female in clear water and
lost her, completely, for four or five seconds, because she turned. They are extremely good
at this. It is the entire thing they do."

The specific hazard on the outer Cape follows from where the seals lie. In South Africa,
white sharks famously hunt seals in deep water at the base of an island, coming up from
below in the dark with enough momentum to leave the surface. Here, the seals haul out on
shallow sandbars, and the sharks come in after them along the troughs between those bars,
which means they hunt routinely in water less than five metres deep and frequently in water
shallower than that.

"That's the local fact," Vasquez says. "Not that there are sharks. There are sharks
everywhere. It's that ours work in water you can stand up in."

---

She says this from the flying bridge of a 25-foot centre console about a mile off the beach,
steering standing, with one bare foot braced on the gunwale. Behind the boat, a crewman is
working a length of bait on a rope, and this is the part of the operation that Vasquez knows
some readers will object to.

"Yes, we use bait," she says, before being asked. "You cannot put a tag on an animal you
cannot get alongside. If somebody has a better idea I will listen to it, and in eleven years
nobody has had one."

The tags are acoustic transmitters, fitted to the dorsal fin or implanted, that emit a coded
ping picked up by receivers moored along the coast. When a tagged shark passes a receiver, it
is logged. The system's great limitation, which Vasquez repeats to every reporter and
watches get dropped from every story, is that it is a presence detector and not an absence
detector.

"A tagged shark shows up when it swims past a receiver," she says. "If it doesn't swim past a
receiver, nothing happens. Nothing happening means nothing. Every single summer, somebody
writes that the detections were down, therefore the sharks were gone. The detections were
down. That is all anybody knows."

Individual animals are identified from the notch pattern on the trailing edge of the dorsal
fin, which is as distinctive as a fingerprint and considerably easier to photograph. Vasquez
has a catalogue running to several hundred animals, some of which she has seen in nine
separate seasons, and she is careful about how she talks about them.

"I don't name them," she says. "The public names them. I understand why and I don't fight it
any more, but a named animal gets a personality, and a personality gets intentions, and
intentions are how you end up with a cull."

What she wants, and cannot get, is time.

"Twenty years. That's what this needs. Twenty years of the same measurements taken the same
way. Everybody funds five, and at five years you can't tell a trend from a wet summer."

Asked about Tavares and the aircraft, she does not hesitate.

"Bobby sees them before the software does. I've stopped pretending that isn't true." Drone
patrols, she says, are useful and improving and cover a rectangle of water; a man who has
watched the same eleven kilometres of beach for three decades is doing something else
entirely. "He knows what that beach looks like when nothing is happening. That's the whole
skill. You can't buy it and you can't hire it in a season."

Tavares puts it less kindly.

"Everybody looks for a shape," he says. "It's not a shape. It's a colour that's wrong, moving
at the wrong speed for the wrong reason." He is scornful of the idea that a sand shadow is
easily confused with an animal. "A sand shadow doesn't accelerate."

The numbers, when you get to them, are almost insultingly small.

Worldwide, unprovoked shark bites run at somewhere between about 60 and 80 a year, of which
roughly five to ten are fatal. Drowning kills orders of magnitude more people at the same
beaches on the same afternoons. On any defensible actuarial basis, a parent standing on the
outer Cape should be worried about the rip current, the sun, the drive home, and then, a
great distance further down the list, the shark.

This is true and it is useless, and everybody who works on this coast knows it is useless.

"You can't hand somebody a statistic while they're watching a helicopter land on the beach,"
says Dana Whitcomb, 55, who administers beaches for one of the outer towns and who declined
to be interviewed twice before agreeing. "It's not that they're stupid. It's that you're
answering a question they didn't ask."

Whitcomb carries a laminated flowchart in her back pocket. It runs to about a dozen boxes
and it governs when a beach closes, for how long, and what goes up the flagpole. She is
happy to show it to anyone.

"Everybody thinks the flowchart is the hard part," she says. "The flowchart is the easy part."

The hard part is a currency she can spend exactly once per season, and she is precise about
what it is.

"If I close it every time anybody sees anything, they stop believing the flag. And the flag
is all I've got. There's no fence. I can't put a fence in the ocean. I have a purple flag,
and a lifeguard with a whistle, and the fact that when we say get out, people get out. That
last thing is the entire safety system on this coast, and it is made of trust, and you can
spend it."

Wendy Corcoran, 58, who has run a snack concession behind one of the beach lots for nineteen
years, can tell you what a closure costs her by eleven in the morning.

"Purple flag day, I'm down about sixty per cent by noon and I don't get it back," she says.
She is not, she is careful to say, arguing for fewer closures. "I'd rather be down sixty per
cent. I'm just telling you it isn't free, and when people say the town's being cautious,
somebody's paying for the caution and it isn't the town."

Meredith Kohl, 34, was bitten in August 2021, in about 1.5 metres of water, roughly 40 metres
off the beach at Sedge Hollow, while standing up and adjusting the strap of a boogie board
she was holding for her nephew.

She remembers a heavy impact against her left leg with no pain attached to it, which she says
is the detail nobody believes, and a sensation of being moved sideways with great force,
"like being hit by a car door, if the car was underneath you". Then it let go.

She has a scar that runs from behind the knee to mid-calf, which she measures against a
tailor's tape for her surgeons every few months, and a functional leg that tires early. The
scar is not what she wants to talk about.

"They want me to say bite," she says. "A dog bites. A mosquito bites. This was not a dog."

She is referring, specifically, to a shift in the professional literature and increasingly in
official communication, away from "shark attack" and toward "shark bite", "shark incident",
or "negative encounter". She has read the papers. She is not dismissive of them.

"I understand what he's trying to do," she says of Sanjay Rele, whose work she has read
closely and whose argument she can restate more fairly than most of his colleagues do. "I
think he should do it with some other word and not with mine."

Rele, 41, is a social scientist who studies the language of risk, and who records every
interview on two devices because, he says, being misquoted on this particular subject is an
occupational certainty.

His argument is not that shark bites are trivial. It is that the phrase "shark attack"
smuggles in a claim about the animal's intention.

"'Attack' is a claim about a mind," he says. "It says: this animal identified you, selected
you, and pursued you. We do not have access to the animal's mind, and every piece of evidence
we do have points somewhere else. In the overwhelming majority of survivable incidents, the
animal releases immediately and does not return. That is not what predation looks like. A
white shark that has decided to eat a seal does not let the seal go."

He can trace the cost of the word, and it is here that his case gets its force. In 1916, a
cluster of bites on the New Jersey shore produced the "rogue shark" theory: the idea that
individual sharks develop a taste for human flesh and must be hunted down. The theory is
long discarded. The policy it justified was not.

"It produced bounties," Rele says. "It produced culls. It produced, for most of a century,
a management assumption that the correct response to a bite is to kill sharks, which does not
reduce risk and does reduce shark populations, and we now have several natural experiments
demonstrating exactly that."

He is careful about Kohl, and he brings her up unprompted.

"I am not telling a survivor what to call what happened to her. I would never. I am telling a
state agency what to put in a press release, and those are different rooms with different
consequences."

Put to Kohl, this distinction does not land.

"It doesn't stay in that room," she says. "It comes out of that room and into the newspaper
and then somebody at a party tells me I wasn't attacked, I was bitten, as though they're
correcting my grammar." She is not angry when she says this; she has clearly said it before.
"And the thing is, I agree with him about the sharks. I don't want anybody killing sharks.
I've said that on television. I just want the word for the thing that happened to me."

There is a version of this article that resolves the disagreement. It would require deciding
whether a word belongs to the person it was used about or to the policy it produces, and
neither the scientific literature nor the survivor community has settled it, and neither will
this magazine.

Forty miles from Sedge Hollow, in the summer of 1974, a film crew spent five difficult
months off Martha's Vineyard shooting a picture about a shark that hunts people. *Jaws* was
released in June 1975 and did more to shape the public understanding of this animal than
every piece of research conducted before or since.

The detail worth holding onto is that there were no white sharks to speak of in that water at
that time. The seals had been shot out. The prey base was gone and the predator had gone with
it. The most influential shark in history was a malfunctioning fibreglass prop in a sea that
had no real ones in it.

Peter Benchley, who wrote the novel, spent much of his later life saying so, and became an
advocate for shark conservation, and repeatedly stated that he could not have written the
book had he known then what was subsequently established about the animals.

The sharks arrived about thirty-five years after the film left.

"That's the part that gets me," Tavares says. "They made the movie here when there was
nothing here. Now there's something here and everybody thinks they already know what it is,
because of the movie."

The alternative to the flowchart is visible on Réunion, in the Indian Ocean, where the
authorities did the thing Cape Cod has so far declined to do.

After a cluster of serious incidents, Réunion in 2013 banned swimming and surfing outside
the lagoon and a small number of supervised areas. The ban has been in place, in varying
forms, ever since.

Marie-Claude Payet, 52, ran a surf school in the west of the island and now runs a shop.

"They banned the ocean," she says. "You can phrase it more politely. That is what they did."

She wants the water reopened and is under no illusions about the risk, which on Réunion is a
different risk with a different animal. The bites there are principally attributed to bull
sharks, *Carcharhinus leucas*, and tiger sharks. Bull sharks tolerate fresh water, frequent
river mouths, and hunt effectively in turbid conditions where visibility is close to nil.

"Here it is not the white," Payet says. "Here it is the bull, and the bull comes to the river
mouth in brown water after rain. It is a different animal and a different problem, and
everybody writes about us as if it is the same problem."

She keeps her son's board in the shop, unrepaired. She does not offer to explain it and is
not asked.

The Réunion comparison cuts in more than one direction, and honest people on Cape Cod use it
both ways. A closure regime works: nobody is bitten in water nobody is in. It also converts a
coastline into a prohibition, permanently, and Réunion is ten years into finding out what
that does to a place.

Cape Cod is a terminal moraine: a long ridge of rubble bulldozed into the Atlantic by the
Laurentide ice sheet and left there when the ice went, perhaps 18,000 years ago. It is
temporary in a way that unsettles people who own houses on it. The outer beach retreats by
metres in some years, and the sandbars that the seals lie on move between seasons.

Onto that impermanent sandbank, for a period considerably longer than there have been people
in North America, grey seals have hauled out and white sharks have come in along the troughs
to take them. The interval when that was not happening — call it the century between the
bounty and the recovery — was the anomaly. It was also, by coincidence, exactly the century
in which Americans developed the habit of swimming in the sea for pleasure.

"You want me to say it's changed," says Gil Sorensen, 66, a retired harbourmaster who
declined twice before talking for most of an afternoon about 1976. "Of course it's changed.
The water's warmer. The seals are back. And there's four times the people. Pick which one of
those you want to be angry about."

At 11:40 on a Tuesday in July, Tomasz Wierzbicki, 29, head lifeguard on a beach that will
have about 900 people on it by two o'clock, gets a radio call from the spotter aircraft and
walks into the water up to his knees with a whistle already in his mouth.

He blows it four times, short. He raises both arms. He says, in a voice pitched to carry
without sounding urgent, "Everybody out of the water, please. Right now, all the way out,
thank you."

Sixty or seventy people come out of the sea. It takes about ninety seconds. A couple of
teenagers dawdle and are moved along by a junior guard of seventeen who does not seem to find
this difficult. Nobody panics. Nobody runs. A woman asks Wierzbicki what it is and he says,
"We'll put the flag up in a minute," and keeps walking.

Only when the water is empty does the purple flag go up.

"I have never said the word on this beach," Wierzbicki says afterwards. "Not once. You say
clear the water, you say now, you say thank you. You tell them why when they're out and dry
and they can hear you."

Above him, at 500 feet, with the window open, Tavares comes back down the beach for the
fourth time that morning and marks the glass again.

The animal he is looking at is about four and a half metres long, is warm-blooded in a cold
sea, can find a heartbeat in the dark, is the reason the seals below her exist in the numbers
they do, and has, as far as anyone can establish, no interest whatsoever in the ninety people
now standing on the sand watching the water she is in.

The flag will come down in an hour. The beach will fill again. The bar will move a little
this winter, and the seals will find it, and so will she.

"Same time tomorrow," Tavares says, and turns north.
