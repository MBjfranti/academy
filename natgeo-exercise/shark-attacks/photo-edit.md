# Photo edit: The Word for What Happened

> **Specified, not shot.** Nothing generated. Invented subjects; see `README.md`.

Eleven frames. The controlling decision is a refusal.

Every published shark edit for fifty years has been built on the same three pictures: the
breach, the open jaws from a cage, and the fin. Those images are the reason the animal is
misunderstood, and running them here would undo the text on the way past. **This edit
contains no breach, no open mouth, no cage, no blood, and no fin cliché.**

The shark is hard to see in almost every frame it appears in, because that is the accurate
representation of the animal and it is also the point of the story: the hazard is invisible,
the management is entirely about people, and the pictures should be about people.

---

## 1. OPENER — full-bleed spread

**Assignment.** Shot from the passenger seat of the Cessna, through the windscreen, at 500
feet. Tavares's grease-pencil circle is on the glass, sharp, in the near foreground. Through
it, small and soft and unmistakably an animal, the shark on the second bar. The beach and
about forty swimmers are in the upper third of the frame.

**Technique.** Focus on the glass, not the water, so the pencil mark is the sharp thing and
the shark is not. Shot handheld through an open-window slipstream at 1/2000 to kill the
vibration, wide enough to hold beach and animal together, which means waiting for the one
pass where they line up.

**Job.** The entire story in one image: a man's mark on a piece of glass, an animal nobody
below can see, and the swimmers. It also states the edit's visual thesis immediately — the
shark is the blurred thing and the human system is the sharp thing.

**Caption.** Bobby Tavares has spotted this coastline for 31 summers. The circle is grease
pencil on the windscreen. The animal is a female of about 4.5 metres, roughly 200 metres from
the nearest swimmer.

---

## 2. Full page — vertical

**Assignment.** Straight-down aerial: a sandbar packed with hauled-out grey seals, the trough
beyond, and in the same frame, at true scale, the swimming area and people in the water.

**Technique.** Drone at altitude for the top-down geometry, midday for minimum shadow, polariser
to cut surface glare and show through into the trough.

**Job.** Establishes the spatial fact the whole piece rests on and that prose struggles with:
the distance between the prey and the people is very small, and both are on the same
photograph at the same scale. No text is needed.

**Caption.** Grey seals haul out on sandbars within metres of swimming beaches. White sharks
hunt them along the troughs between bars, routinely in water less than five metres deep.

---

## 3. Half page

**Assignment.** Nora Aiken at the spotting scope on the dune at first light, counting, mouth
slightly open mid-count.

**Technique.** 300mm from behind and below so the dune grass runs across the bottom of the
frame, backlit, seals as a soft dark band beyond.

**Job.** Puts a working scientist in the landscape and shows that the seal recovery is
measured, laboriously, by a person on a hill.

**Caption.** Aiken has counted this haul-out for 11 summers. Grey seals were reduced to a few
hundred animals in New England before federal protection in 1972.

---

## 4. Small inset — specimen

**Assignment.** Macro of the ampullae of Lorenzini: the pore field on a white shark's snout,
lit to show the pits as pits.

**Technique.** Museum specimen access, not a live animal. Raking single source almost parallel
to the skin, focus-stacked across the curvature. **Access note:** this frame requires a
preserved head in a collection and a curator willing to let it out of fluid for an hour, which
is the hardest booking in the edit.

**Job.** Makes the sensory mechanism physical. The text explains that the animal can find a
heartbeat in the dark; this shows the hardware that does it.

**Caption.** Hundreds of jelly-filled pores, the ampullae of Lorenzini, detect the minute
electrical fields generated by contracting muscle. At close range they work when vision does
not.

---

## 5. Full page

**Assignment.** Vasquez's hands and forearms over the gunwale, wet, working a tag applicator
against a flank of shark that fills the bottom of the frame — skin texture, not teeth. Her
face is not in it.

**Technique.** Shot from a boarding platform at water level, 24mm, close, in flat overcast so
nothing blows out. The animal's head is deliberately outside the frame.

**Job.** The counter-image to every shark photograph ever published. All surface, no mouth.
It also shows the physical reality of tagging, including the proximity, which is the thing
critics of the bait argument never picture accurately.

**Caption.** Tagging requires the animal alongside the boat. "If somebody has a better idea I
will listen to it," Vasquez says. "In eleven years nobody has had one."

---

## 6. Small — unglamorous on purpose

**Assignment.** An acoustic receiver being serviced: a cylinder of biofouled plastic on a
mooring line, barnacles, a laptop in a dry bag.

**Technique.** Straight documentation, on-camera fill, no styling. Should look like plumbing.

**Job.** Deflates the technology. Readers imagine a surveillance network; the network is a
pipe on a rope that only knows a shark was there if it swam past.

**Caption.** Receivers log a tagged shark only when it passes within range. Non-detection is
not absence — a distinction that vanishes from most reporting.

---

## 7. Half page

**Assignment.** Whitcomb's laminated flowchart, half out of the back pocket of her uniform
shorts, in situ, beach behind.

**Technique.** Waist-level, tight, available light. She is walking away.

**Job.** Comic and serious at once. The entire safety apparatus of a coastline is a laminated
sheet in a pocket, and she says so.

**Caption.** "Everybody thinks the flowchart is the hard part," says beach administrator Dana
Whitcomb. "The flowchart is the easy part."

---

## 8. Full page — specimen treatment, no face

**Assignment.** Meredith Kohl's left calf with the tailor's tape laid along the scar, on a
plain ground, shot the way an object is shot.

**Technique.** Studio-clean single raking source, dark falloff, sharp end to end. Deliberately
the same lighting grammar as frame 4, so the reader registers that a person's leg is being
given the visual treatment of a museum specimen.

**Job.** This is the frame that earns the terminology section. Rele's argument is about press
releases; this is what the argument is about for one person. Photographing it as a specimen
is uncomfortable, and it should be, because that discomfort is precisely Kohl's complaint.

**Caption.** Kohl was bitten in about 1.5 metres of water in August 2021. She measures the
scar against a tailor's tape for her surgeons. "They want me to say bite," she says. "A dog
bites."

---

## 9. DOUBLE-PAGE SPREAD — the decisive moment

**Assignment.** Wierzbicki knee-deep, arms raised, whistle in his mouth, and sixty or seventy
people walking out of the sea toward the camera. The purple flag is **not yet up** — it goes
up after the water is empty, and the frame has to catch that ninety-second gap.

**Technique.** From the waterline, slightly low, 35mm, so the crowd fills the frame and the
horizon sits high. One chance per event. Requires being on the beach when the radio call
comes, which means many wasted days.

**Job.** The whole management thesis in one picture: nobody is running, nobody is panicking,
and the only functioning safety system on this coast is that when a man says get out, people
get out. The absence of the flag is the fact the caption exists to explain.

**Caption.** The flag goes up only after the water is clear. "You say clear the water, you say
now, you say thank you," Wierzbicki says. "You tell them why when they're out and dry."

---

## 10. Half page

**Assignment.** Payet in her shop on Réunion, her son's unrepaired board on the wall behind
her, out of focus.

**Technique.** 85mm, window light from one side, no fill, shot at the end of a conversation
rather than the start.

**Job.** Opens the comparison without the text having to labour it, and holds the piece's one
irreducible human cost. The board is never explained in the caption or the body, deliberately.

**Caption.** Réunion banned swimming and surfing outside supervised areas in 2013. The bites
there are attributed mainly to bull sharks, which hunt in turbid water at river mouths.

---

## 11. CLOSER — full page

**Assignment.** The same beach as frame 9, forty minutes later. Purple flag up. Water
completely empty and completely flat. About ninety people on the sand, all facing the sea.
Nothing visible in the water at all.

**Technique.** Long lens from the dune, compressed, so flag, crowd and empty water stack.
Shot at the same focal length and position as frame 9 for a direct visual rhyme.

**Job.** Returns to the opening third and leaves it open. There is nothing to see, which is
what a shark story actually looks like almost all of the time, and the crowd is looking
anyway.

**Caption.** The flag comes down after an hour and the beach fills again. The sandbar will
move over the winter. The seals will find it, and so will she.

---

## Rhythm

Spread, page, half, inset, page, small, half, page, **spread**, half, page.

One double-page spread inside the body, at frame 9, and it is a picture of people walking out
of the sea. Placing the story's only spread on an image with no shark in it is the edit's
loudest editorial statement.

## What the edit refuses, and why

- **No breach.** It is the most reproduced shark image in the world and it is a South African
  hunting behaviour, not a Cape Cod one. Running it would be factually misleading here.
- **No open jaws, no cage.** Both are pictures of a shark reacting to bait and a diver, which
  is to say pictures of photography, not of sharks.
- **No fin cutting the surface.** It is a film still, not a report.
- **No wound photography beyond frame 8**, which is consented, framed as specimen, and
  faceless.

If the desk demands one conventional shark portrait, it runs small and underwater and calm,
and it runs *after* frame 8, where a reader has been given the means to look at it properly.
