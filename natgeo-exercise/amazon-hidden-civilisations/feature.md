# The Country Under the Canopy

### For a century, archaeology said the Amazon could not hold a civilisation. Then somebody pointed a laser at it

> account are real and appear as published background.

---

Wilder Tapia will not take off, and he explains why for the third time without any sign of
irritation, because the argument happens every morning and he has decided it is part of the
job.

"Cloud base under nine hundred metres, we do not fly," he says. "Everybody wants to have this
conversation on the ground. Nobody wants to have it in the air."

It is 5:50 a.m. at a grass strip in the Bolivian lowlands and the sky over the savanna is
the colour of a wet newspaper. Tapia, 47, is a survey pilot, which means that for six weeks a
year he flies a small twin aircraft in parallel tracks, low and slow, back and forth across
the same rectangle of country, at a constant height and a constant speed, like a man mowing a
very large lawn very carefully. In the cabin behind him, bolted to a frame over an open port
in the floor, is an instrument that costs more than the aeroplane.

By seven the base has lifted. By seven-forty they are on the first line.

Paola Céspedes, 44, the archaeologist who put the survey together, is not on board. She is at
a table in the shade of a hangar with a laptop, a thermos, and the previous day's block
loading at a speed that visibly annoys her.

"We flew it because it was cheap that month," she says. "Not because I was clever. I want to
be honest about that in your article. There was a contractor already in the country doing a
pipeline corridor, and we got the aircraft for eleven days at a price that will never happen
again."

---

The instrument is a lidar unit, and the first thing everyone who works with one wants to
correct is the way it is described.

"The laser does not see through the canopy," says Tobias Reinholt, 51, the remote sensing
specialist, who wears the same orange headset in the aircraft and on the ground and does not
appear to notice when he still has it on at dinner. "Everybody writes see through. It does
not see through anything. It is not an X-ray."

What it does is fire a great many pulses of light straight down — hundreds of thousands per
second — and time how long each one takes to come back. Most of them hit a leaf, and bounce,
and that return records the height of a leaf. But a single pulse can produce several returns
as it clips through gaps: the top of the canopy, then a branch, then understorey, and, if it
happens to thread a hole all the way down, the ground.

The ground return is the whole point, and it is rare.

"First return is the treetop," Reinholt says. "Last return, if you are lucky, is dirt. In
closed forest maybe two or three percent of your pulses make it to the floor. Everything I do
for a living is throwing away ninety-seven percent of the data and keeping the crumbs."

The crumbs, assembled, become a point cloud: tens of gigabytes of individual dots in three
dimensions. Filter the cloud for last returns, discard everything that is obviously
vegetation, interpolate a surface through what remains, and you have a bare-earth model of
the ground beneath a forest nobody has walked.

Then you light it. Not with real light, but with a synthetic sun placed at a very low angle
in the software, throwing long artificial shadows across the model. It is the same trick as
raking light across a coin.

"And that," Reinholt says, "is when everybody in the room stops talking."

Bamboo, he adds, defeats the whole procedure, and he has strong feelings about bamboo.

For most of the twentieth century, the professional position was that there was nothing under
there to find.

The reasoning was environmental and it was not stupid. Amazonian soils are, in general,
ancient, deeply weathered, acidic and poor in phosphorus. Clear a patch and burn it and the
ash will give you three good years, after which the plot is exhausted and you move. A system
like that supports mobile people in small groups. It does not, the argument ran, support
cities, and it certainly does not support the kind of surplus that lets some people build
monuments while other people grow food.

The most influential statement of that position came from the American archaeologist Betty
Meggers, whose 1971 book characterised the region as a "counterfeit paradise": magnificent to
look at, and biologically incapable of carrying complex society.

This had a consequence for the historical record. In 1541 and 1542 the expedition of
Francisco de Orellana descended the river, and its chronicler, the Dominican friar Gaspar de
Carvajal, described something that did not match: settlement running along the bank almost
continuously for days at a time, roads leading inland, defended positions, and populations
large enough to put fleets of canoes on the water.

For four hundred years, that account was treated as a lie.

It was a comfortable conclusion, and it was self-confirming, because everybody who went back
to check found the river nearly empty. What they did not adequately account for is that
Old World disease travels faster than Old World people. Epidemics moved along the river
network ahead of the travellers who later reported finding nobody there, which means the
Amazon was described, at length and in detail, in a condition it had only recently been put
into.

Lidar has made the twentieth-century position untenable in about six years. What the laser
shows is earthworks at a scale nobody was looking for, because nobody thought to look:
raised platforms, ranked causeways running dead straight across ground that floods, canals,
reservoirs. And the soil beneath the trees turns out, in places, to have been manufactured.

The argument now is not whether people were here. It is how many, what to call what they
built, and who has standing to say.

---

By mid-morning Céspedes has driven forty minutes and walked twenty to a place she wants
shown rather than described.

The walk is through scrubby forest on flat ground. It is hot. There is a slight rise
underfoot, then a slighter fall, then a rise again, and after a while a low bank that could
be anything.

"You are standing on it," she says. "You have been standing on it for about ten minutes."

Overhead the canopy is unbroken and the horizon is thirty metres away in every direction. On
the hillshaded model on her laptop, the same ground is a plan: a platform roughly rectangular
and unmistakably deliberate, a second terrace stepped above it, a moat on three sides, and
causeways leaving the complex in four directions like spokes, running arrow-straight into the
savanna for kilometres.

"Twenty-six square kilometres in this cluster," she says. "Ranked platforms. Conical mounds
on the summits. Water management. A tiered pattern, so you have two big centres and then
secondary sites and then small ones, arranged in a way that is difficult to explain unless
somebody was arranging it."

The culture is called Casarabe and its dates run roughly from AD 500 to 1400, which is to say
that this was occupied while Charlemagne was alive and was still occupied when Chaucer was.
The causeways are the detail that persuades even cautious people, because a causeway is
useless to a small mobile group. It is infrastructure. It is built once, at a cost, by
people who intend to be there in fifty years, and it is maintained by people who intend the
same.

From the ground, all of it is a hill with trees on it.

---

Ignacio Yuvané, 67, is a Mojeño community authority who walks the platform barefoot, which he
says is not ceremonial.

"You cannot feel it in boots," he says.

He is right, in a way that is briefly humiliating for everybody else present. Walking slowly
with his weight forward, he stops twice on ground that reads as flat, and each time there is
a break in slope of perhaps fifteen centimetres that turns out, when the survey team later
checks it against the model, to be a terrace edge the automatic classification had smoothed
away as noise.

His view of the survey is warm and entirely unsentimental.

"You call it a site," he says. "We call it where the old people lived. Those are not the same
sentence."

He grew up being told which ground not to plant on. "My grandmother told me the raised
roads," he says. "She said, do not plant on the roads, the roads are not for that. She did not
have a laser."

He is glad of the survey, he says twice, unprompted, and he does not want the compliment
mistaken for agreement.

"I am glad you can see it now," he says. "I want you to notice that being able to see it is
what made it real for you. It was real before."

---

Forty kilometres away, on a cut bank above a river, Hélène Marchetti, 39, is standing in a
soil pit with a trowel, and the wall beside her is doing something that soil in this region is
not supposed to do.

The top eighty centimetres are black. Not dark brown: black, sharply distinct from the
red-yellow material below and beside it, and studded visibly with fragments of charcoal and
small orange flecks that turn out under a hand lens to be broken pottery.

"Taste it," she says, and holds out a pinch.

It is offered seriously. Soil scientists do taste soil, for texture rather than flavour,
rolling it against the roof of the mouth to feel grit against silt against clay. It tastes of
almost nothing and feels faintly greasy.

This is *terra preta de índio*, Amazonian dark earth, and it is the most quietly astonishing
thing in this story.

Ordinary soil here is a poor medium and has been for millions of years. Warm, wet, and
extremely old, it has been leached of its nutrients, and what remains binds phosphorus in
forms plants struggle to take up. Terra preta is the opposite: fertile, high in nutrients,
and above all high in charcoal.

The charcoal is what does the work. Burnt organic matter forms a rigid, extremely porous
carbon skeleton that resists decay for millennia, and that structure behaves in soil like a
sponge with an electrical charge. It holds cations — calcium, magnesium, potassium — that
would otherwise wash straight through in the rain, and its pore network houses a microbial
community very different from that of the surrounding forest floor.

Mix that charcoal with bone, fish waste, broken pots, ash and household rubbish, over
generations, in the place where you live, and you eventually get soil that is better than
what you started with.

"That is the part people find hard," Marchetti says. "This was not made on purpose. Nobody sat
down in 400 BC and designed a soil amendment programme. This is what the ground under a town
turns into if the town stays put for long enough and burns its waste."

And it lasts. The dark earths are still fertile. Farmers work them today, without adding
anything, on plots where surrounding ground would be exhausted in three seasons.

There is also evidence, contested and under study, that in some conditions the horizon
regenerates — that the black layer deepens over time rather than depleting.

"We do not fully understand why it does that," Marchetti says. "That is the most interesting
sentence available in my field and I am aware it sounds like an admission."

The implication runs backwards. Terra preta occurs where people lived, in the quantity they
lived, for as long as they lived there. It is a settlement record written in dirt. And it
means that the poor-soil argument, which was the entire basis for saying complex society was
impossible here, was describing a condition that people in Amazonia had locally and
repeatedly solved.

Rosana Kaxuyana, 35, an ethnobotanist, takes the argument up into the trees.

"Brazil nut does not grow like that by itself," she says, in a stand where *Bertholletia
excelsa* is far commoner than it has any ecological business being. "Neither does peach palm.
Peach palm is fully domesticated. There is no wild version that looks like this. Somebody
planted it, and somebody's grandchildren planted more."

Dozens of Amazonian tree species show signs of long-term human selection and management, and
they cluster near archaeological sites at frequencies that are hard to explain any other way.
A forest that looks primeval can be, in part, an orchard that has been left alone for five
hundred years.

She names three trees before she names the person standing next to her, and she is precise
about why this matters beyond botany.

"The word pristine is doing a great deal of work," she says, "and most of it is being done
against us. If this forest is untouched wilderness, then people are a problem in it. If it is
partly something people made, then we are part of how it got here, and that is a different
legal conversation."

At which point it is necessary to introduce Duncan Ferreira-Blake, 62, who has read
everything above and accepts most of it and is currently the least popular archaeologist in
this particular corner of the discipline.

He annotates other people's published figures in red pen and posts them back to their
authors, which he describes as collegial and his colleagues describe variously.

"I accept the earthworks," he says. "Every single one of them. I have never in my life
disputed an earthwork. Please put that high up, because it always gets cut."

What he disputes is what is being calculated from them.

"A monument tells you about labour organised over time," he says. "A big platform means that
some quantity of people moved some quantity of earth over some number of years, and that
somebody was coordinating it. Those are three unknowns in one observation. To get a
population figure out of it you must assume a construction period, and a labour rate, and a
proportion of the population engaged, and a settlement density, and then you multiply.
Change any one of those assumptions by a factor of two and your regional total moves by an
order of magnitude. I have seen numbers published this decade that differ by a factor of
ten, and they are drawn from the same data."

He is careful to say he is not defending Meggers, whose position he calls "wrong, and wrong
for bad reasons".

"The old view was that these people could not have done this. That view is dead and it
deserved to die. I am making a much smaller argument, which is that the arithmetic replacing
it should be checked with the same energy that was used to demolish the thing it replaced."

He is aware of his role.

"I am now the villain of a very good story," he says. "I would still like the sums seen to."

Put to Céspedes, this produces something close to agreement and then a sharp turn.

"Duncan is right that I cannot count people," she says. "I have said so in print. He is wrong
that it does not matter how much earth they moved. You do not build eleven kilometres of
raised causeway across a floodplain to serve forty families. I cannot give you a headcount
and I can tell you it was not forty families."

Neither of them expects this to be resolved soon, and neither of them says so with any
distress. This magazine cannot settle it either.

What is settled is that the record is being lost while the argument runs.

Milton Arze, 58, runs cattle on savanna about an hour from the survey block, and he raises the
subject himself before he can be asked about it, in the flat tone of a man who has decided
that lying would be more trouble.

"I took the top off two of them," he says. "I will tell you because you will find out anyway.
The tractor was rented by the day and the rain was coming."

He is not remorseful, exactly, and he is not defiant either.

"Nobody told me it was anything," he says. "It is a hill in my field. There is no sign, there
is no paper, nobody came. If somebody comes and says this is protected, fine, that is a
different situation. Nobody came."

He is describing the actual mechanism of loss, which is not malice but paperwork. An earthwork
has no legal protection until it is recorded, and recording is slow, and half the year the
ground is under water and nobody can work.

In a regional town four hours away, Yara Simões, 56, who directs a museum, declined to be
interviewed twice and then unlocked a store room.

"You want to photograph the collection," she says. "There is no collection. There are boxes."

There are about eleven thousand potsherds in the room, in banana crates, on metal shelving,
under a dehumidifier that stopped working in March.

"Every season somebody brings me more," she says. "I am not ungrateful. I am telling you that
the thing everybody is excited about produces, at the end, a very large number of broken pots
in a hot room, and no minister has ever once been photographed standing in a hot room."

---

Nothing in this story is a discovery in the sense the word is usually used.

The mounds have been there since the seventh century. The causeways were built by people who
expected them to last and were correct. The dark earth has been under the trees since before
Rome fell, and the people who live nearby have been farming it, deliberately, for as long as
anybody can remember, on the entirely accurate grounds that it grows things better.

What happened in the last six years is that an instrument made it legible to a particular
group of people who required it to be legible in a particular way.

At dusk, on a causeway that runs off the platform in a line so straight it looks surveyed,
Yuvané walks a few hundred metres out into the grass, barefoot, and stops where the raised
bank drops back to the flood plain and the road, if that is what it is, disappears under
seasonal water.

He points along the line with his chin.

"It goes to the other one," he says. "Everybody knows that. You will find it with your
machine and then you will write that it was found."

He is smiling when he says it, which does not make it less pointed.

Behind him the causeway runs back toward the trees, dead straight, holding the crown it was
given about a thousand years ago, doing the job it was built for on ground that floods every
year and has flooded every year since, waiting for somebody to notice that it never stopped
working.
