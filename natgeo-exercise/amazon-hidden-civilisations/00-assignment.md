## Step 1. The peg and the nut graf

**Why now.** Airborne lidar has, in about six years, overturned a century of assumption
about how many people lived in Amazonia before 1492 and what they built. Surveys in the
Llanos de Mojos in Bolivia revealed Casarabe-culture settlements with monumental platform
mounds, causeways and reservoirs under forest that looked untouched. Surveys in the Upano
valley in Ecuador revealed thousands of platforms and a road network older than most of what
survives in Europe. The instrument did not find new sites so much as make visible a
landscape that was always there and could not be seen from inside it.

**Nut graf.** *(target: paragraph 7-9)*

> For most of the twentieth century the professional consensus was that Amazonia could not
> have supported complex society, because its soils are poor and its rivers flood, and that
> the sixteenth-century Spanish accounts of continuous riverside towns were the lies of men
> justifying a failed expedition. Lidar has made that position untenable in about six years.
> What the laser shows is earthworks on a scale nobody was looking for: raised platforms,
> ranked causeways, canals cut kilometres across seasonally flooded savanna. And the soil
> under the forest turns out in places to have been manufactured. The argument now is not
> whether people were here. It is how many, what to call what they built, and who gets to
> say.

**The unresolved disagreement:** Céspedes and Ferreira-Blake, on population. Céspedes reads
the causeway networks and reservoir volumes as evidence of dense, coordinated, permanently
settled populations in the high hundreds of thousands regionally. Ferreira-Blake accepts
every earthwork and rejects every extrapolation from it, on the grounds that monumental
construction indexes labour organisation over time, not headcount at a moment, and that the
big regional numbers now circulating are arithmetic performed on assumptions. **Both are
careful people. The piece does not adjudicate.**

Second, softer disagreement: whether "city" is the right word for low-density agrarian
urbanism. Céspedes says yes and finds the objection provincial. Ferreira-Blake says the word
imports Old World expectations. Yuvané thinks the whole argument is people naming his
grandmother's country in a language she did not speak.

**Local expert outranking the credentialled one:** Yuvané walks a mound barefoot and finds
terrace breaks the point cloud missed, twice.

**Reluctant source:** Yara Simões.

## Step 3b. Fact dossier (71 items; ~a third used)

**[soft]** marks anything I would verify before publication.

**Lidar mechanism**
1. Lidar: pulsed laser from an aircraft, timing the return to measure distance.
2. Systems fire on the order of hundreds of thousands of pulses per second.
3. A single pulse can produce multiple returns: canopy, understorey, ground.
4. Bare-earth models are built from last returns, filtered from everything above.
5. In dense canopy only a small percentage of pulses reach the ground.
6. Bamboo and dense understorey defeat it badly.
7. The output is a point cloud, then a digital elevation model.
8. Hillshading a DEM from a low artificial sun angle is what makes earthworks visible.
9. Features invisible from the ground appear because the eye needs the whole plan at once.
10. Survey cost is dominated by flying hours, so weather windows govern everything.
11. Cloud base and rain stop flights outright.
12. Lidar finds topography, not artefacts; it cannot date anything.
13. Ground truthing by excavation remains mandatory.

**The Bolivian and Ecuadorian findings**
14. Llanos de Mojos: seasonally flooded savanna in the Bolivian Amazon.
15. Casarabe culture, roughly AD 500 to 1400.
16. Lidar there revealed platform mounds, conical pyramids, moats and causeways **[soft on detail]**.
17. Two large settlement sites, Cotoca and Landívar, anchor a tiered network.
18. Causeways run for kilometres, dead straight, across flood-prone ground.
19. Canals and reservoirs indicate deliberate water management.
20. The settlement pattern is tiered: large centres, secondary sites, smaller sites.
21. Upano valley, Ecuador: lidar revealed thousands of platforms and connecting roads.
22. Upano occupation dates run from roughly 500 BC to AD 300-600 **[soft]**.
23. Some Upano roads are wide, straight, and sunken between banks.
24. Both findings were published in major journals within two years of each other.

**Terra preta**
25. *Terra preta de índio*: Amazonian dark earth.
26. Distinguished by high pyrogenic carbon (charcoal) content.
27. Also contains bone, pottery sherds, and domestic organic waste.
28. High cation exchange capacity relative to surrounding oxisols.
29. Surrounding soils are ancient, leached, acidic, low in phosphorus.
30. Slash-and-burn on ordinary soil yields well for about three years.
31. Terra preta remains fertile after centuries and is farmed today.
32. Some evidence suggests the soil regenerates in depth over time **[soft, contested]**.
33. Charcoal's porous structure hosts microbial communities and holds nutrients.
34. Terra preta is typically found near rivers, on settlement sites.
35. It is, in effect, a by-product of long-term habitation.
36. Estimates of total terra preta area in Amazonia vary by an order of magnitude **[soft]**.

**The old argument**
37. Francisco de Orellana descended the Amazon in 1541-42.
38. Gaspar de Carvajal's account describes continuous riverside settlement.
39. It describes towns, roads inland, and defended positions.
40. The account was widely dismissed as exaggeration or fabrication.
41. Later travellers found sparse populations, apparently confirming the dismissal.
42. Introduced disease preceded most later European travel inland.
43. Depopulation may have removed most of the population before it was described **[soft].**
44. Betty Meggers argued environmental limits capped Amazonian social complexity.
45. Her 1971 book framed the region as a "counterfeit paradise".
46. Anna Roosevelt's Marajó Island work challenged that from the 1980s onward.
47. The dispute was long, public, and unusually bitter for the discipline.
48. Population estimates for pre-1492 Amazonia range from about 1 million to over 10 million **[soft]**.

**The forest as artefact**
49. Brazil nut, *Bertholletia excelsa*; peach palm, *Bactris gasipaes*; cacao, *Theobroma cacao*.
50. Around 85 tree species show signs of domestication or management **[soft]**.
51. Domesticated species are significantly over-represented near archaeological sites.
52. Brazil nut stands are frequently associated with former settlement.
53. Peach palm is fully domesticated and does not occur wild in its cultivated form.
54. The "pristine forest" framing underpins much conservation law and rhetoric.
55. If the forest is partly an artefact, that framing is factually wrong.
56. Indigenous groups have made this argument for decades without instrument backing.

**Practical, institutional, sensory**
57. Regional museums frequently lack climate control and storage.
58. Sherds are bulk material; a single season can generate tens of thousands.
59. Cattle ranching is the dominant land conversion pressure in the Bolivian lowlands.
60. Levelling a mound with a tractor takes hours.
61. Earthworks have no legal protection unless recorded, and recording is slow.
62. Seasonal flooding makes half the year unworkable on the ground.
63. Mounds are visible from the ground only as low tree-covered rises.
64. Walking a causeway is easiest barefoot; the terrace breaks are felt, not seen.
65. Survey aircraft fly low, slow, and in parallel tracks like mowing a lawn.
66. Point cloud files run to tens of gigabytes per survey block.
67. Field assistants are hired seasonally and released at the end of a contract.
68. River towns keep pottery in domestic shrines, on shelves with saints' figures.
69. Mosquito density in the wet season effectively halts night work.
70. Terra preta is visibly black against the surrounding red-yellow soil.
71. The smell of terra preta is distinctly different: richer, more like garden compost.

## Step 4. The chain

> Amazonia looked pristine, and its soils are genuinely poor → so archaeology concluded that
> complex society was impossible here → so Carvajal's 1542 account of continuous riverside
> towns was dismissed for four centuries as a liar's report → and the dismissal was
> self-confirming, because disease had emptied the river before anyone went back to check →
> then lidar arrived and digitally removed the canopy → revealing platform mounds, ranked
> causeways and reservoirs across ground nobody had surveyed → and the soil under the trees
> turned out in places to be manufactured, made of charcoal and kitchen waste and two
> thousand years → so the forest itself is partly an artefact of the people who lived in it
> → which makes "pristine wilderness" factually wrong and legally load-bearing → and the
> descendants of the people who built it have been saying so, without instruments, the whole
> time.

**Reorder test:** the terra preta section cannot precede the poor-soils section; the whole
point is that the soil is an exception somebody made.
