# Assignment: protecting historical sites in a warzone

> Imagined feature. **All quoted speech, all named sources, the town and the museum are
> invented.** The 1954 Hague Convention, the Blue Shield emblem, satellite damage
> verification and the 2012-13 Timbuktu manuscript evacuation are documented public record.
> No real death is fictionalised and no real person is quoted. See `README.md`.

## Peg

The law says mark your monuments. The 1954 Hague Convention gives cultural property a
protective emblem, a blue and white shield, on the reasoning that an attacker who can see
what a building is will spare it. That reasoning assumes an enemy who is trying not to hit
it. Where heritage is targeted deliberately, the emblem stops being a shield and becomes a
map, and the people responsible for these buildings have quietly inverted seventy years of
doctrine: conceal, disperse, tell nobody, move at night.

## Nut graf *(paragraph 6-9)*

> Every protective measure available to a museum in a war is a trade against evidence.
> Sandbags stop fragments and not blast. Moving a collection saves it from one strike and
> exposes it to a road. Concealing a site keeps it off a targeting list and forfeits the
> legal protection that marking was supposed to buy, and makes the damage far harder to prove
> to a court afterwards. Nobody staffing a regional museum was trained for any of this, and
> the decisions cannot be deferred, because the trade has to be made before anything happens
> rather than after.

**Unresolved disagreement:** Zabuzhko and Krylenko on marking. Zabuzhko wants every site
registered and emblemed, because the legal protection is real, the register is what a
prosecution runs on, and an unmarked site struck is a site nobody can prove was protected.
Krylenko will not mark, because in this war the emblem has been followed by the strike often
enough that her staff no longer believe in it, and because she would rather hold the icons
than the judgment. **Both are right. The piece does not settle it.**

**Second tension, deliberately not resolved:** Kobza, the deputy mayor, thinks the whole
enterprise is misallocated while the town needs boilers. He is given his best argument.

**Local expert outranking the credentialled one:** Marchuk, the guard, knows which cellar
stays dry, which the survey did not.

## Fact dossier (61 items)

`[soft]` marks anything I would verify.

**Law and emblem**
1. Hague Convention for the Protection of Cultural Property in the Event of Armed Conflict, 1954.
2. It followed the destruction of the Second World War and was the first treaty dedicated to the subject.
3. It establishes a distinctive emblem: a blue and white shield.
4. Single emblem indicates general protection; triple emblem indicates special protection **[soft]**.
5. A Second Protocol, 1999, created a category of enhanced protection and individual criminal responsibility.
6. Deliberate attack on cultural property can constitute a war crime.
7. The International Criminal Court has convicted for destruction of cultural heritage **[soft on detail]**.
8. Protection depends on the property not being used for military purposes.
9. Marking is voluntary in practice and unevenly applied.
10. Blue Shield International is the NGO named for the emblem.
11. Registers of protected sites are maintained nationally and shared with belligerents **[soft]**.

**The inversion**
12. Marking assumes an adversary attempting to avoid the site.
13. Where heritage is targeted, the emblem identifies a target.
14. Practitioners in several conflicts have declined to mark for this reason **[soft]**.
15. Concealment forfeits the evidentiary benefit of registration.
16. Prosecution requires proving the property's protected status and the attacker's knowledge.
17. Unregistered damaged buildings are evidentially weak cases.

**Physical protection**
18. Sandbagging protects against fragmentation, not overpressure.
19. A near-miss blast wave will still destroy an unsupported window or vault.
20. Scaffolding plus geotextile plus sand is a common monument-wrapping method **[soft]**.
21. Stained glass is removed panel by panel and stored flat.
22. Statues too heavy to move are boxed in situ and packed.
23. Sand absorbs moisture and can hold it against stone, causing damage over months.
24. Protective measures left up too long become a conservation problem of their own.

**Moving a collection**
25. Panel paintings are highly sensitive to relative humidity change.
26. Rapid RH change causes the wooden support to move against the paint layer, producing cracking and lifting.
27. Target museum RH is around 50 percent; target temperature around 19°C.
28. Uninsulated cellars in winter can sit near 4°C; roof spaces in summer well above 40°C.
29. Vibration in transit is a principal cause of loss in evacuation.
30. Objects are wrapped, crated, and immobilised inside the crate rather than the crate being cushioned **[soft]**.
31. Salt mines and deep cellars have been used as wartime stores historically.
32. Evacuation concentrates risk: one convoy, one road, one strike.
33. Dispersal reduces catastrophic loss and multiplies administrative risk.
34. Objects moved without paperwork are objects that can disappear legitimately or otherwise.
35. Double-signature release procedures are standard practice and frequently abandoned under pressure.

**Documentation and verification**
36. Satellite damage assessment compares before-and-after high-resolution imagery.
37. Analysts look for roof collapse, debris fields, crater signatures and shadow changes.
38. Cloud cover, revisit intervals and sun angle all delay usable pairs.
39. Verification of a single site can take weeks.
40. Imagery shows structural damage and cannot show contents.
41. UNESCO maintains verified counts of damaged cultural sites and these lag reported counts substantially.
42. Photogrammetric survey before damage is the single most valuable record and is rarely complete.
43. Community photographs are increasingly used as pre-damage baseline **[soft]**.
44. 3D scanning of a facade can be done in a day with a handheld unit.

**Timbuktu precedent**
45. In 2012-13, manuscripts were evacuated from Timbuktu during the occupation of northern Mali.
46. The operation moved a very large number of manuscripts, commonly cited at around 350,000.
47. They were moved in footlockers, by road and by river, over months.
48. Secrecy was central to the operation's success.
49. Some manuscripts were nonetheless burned.
50. Manuscripts subsequently suffered from humidity in Bamako, a different climate from Timbuktu.
51. The saving created a long-term conservation problem in its own right.

**The town, the practicalities**
52. Regional museums typically hold far more than they display.
53. Inventories are frequently incomplete, and wartime is when that becomes visible.
54. Generators, fuel and dehumidifiers compete directly with domestic heating for scarce supply.
55. Volunteer drivers are central to most evacuations and are rarely insured.
56. Curfews restrict movement to specific hours.
57. Vehicles move at night with lights off in some areas.
58. Ordnance disposal must clear a damaged site before conservators can enter.
59. Engineer priorities and conservation priorities routinely conflict.
60. Museum staff in conflict are overwhelmingly women in this region **[soft]**.
61. Winter is the operational constraint on everything.
