The inventory of the museum is in a school exercise book, squared paper, 48 pages, and Oksana
Krylenko carries it in her bag.

She is the director, and she has run this collection for 26 years. The book has been the real
record since the ninth day of the war, when the electricity went for 61 hours and the
database went with it, and it now holds 3,140 lines in four hands. Each line is an object, a
crate number, a destination and two initials. The destinations are written in a shorthand
that Krylenko devised herself and has not explained to anybody outside four people.

"When this is over I will be criticised for this book," she says. "By somebody who was not
here."

I was four hours at a checkpoint 40 kilometres out, and the condition of the visit, agreed in
advance and in writing, was that I would not name the town, photograph any exterior that
identified it, or describe the route. I agreed to all three. I want that said early, because
it is the subject of the article and not a caveat on it: almost everybody in this story is
making a version of the same trade, and mine was the cheapest one available.

## The emblem as a map

In 1954, in the wreckage the Second World War left across Europe, states signed the Hague
Convention for the Protection of Cultural Property in the Event of Armed Conflict. It was the
first treaty devoted to the subject, and among other things it gave cultural property a
distinctive emblem: a shield, pointed below, blue and white.

The logic is plain and it is the logic of a road sign. An attacker who can see what a
building is will spare it. Mark the museum, the cathedral, the archive, register them with
the other side, and the marking does the work.

That logic rests on one assumption, which is that the adversary is trying not to hit the
building. Where that holds, the emblem is the cheapest protection available to a museum:
fabric, paint, a registration form. Where it does not hold, each of those becomes a
publication, and the cheapness runs the other way: paint and a form are also the cheapest
way to tell somebody what a building contains.

"The convention was written by people imagining a different enemy," Krylenko says.

She will not mark her sites. Not the museum, not the store, not the two dependencies. She
has had the emblems since 2015, four of them, and they are in a cupboard in her office in the
original packaging, which she showed me without being asked and then put back.

This is not a local eccentricity. Talk to enough people doing this work and a quiet inversion
of seventy years of doctrine emerges: conceal rather than mark, disperse rather than
consolidate, move at night, keep the list on paper, tell nobody where anything went. The
protective emblem, in a war where cultural sites are hit deliberately, is understood by the
people it was designed to protect as a target indicator.

A ministry official who would speak only unattributably was blunt about why she would not put
her name to that sentence.

"I am not being coy," she said. "If I am named, the next building is on a list. I would
rather be useless in your article than useful to them."

---

Every protective measure available to a museum in a war is a trade against evidence.

Sandbags stop fragments and not blast. Moving a collection saves it from one strike and
exposes it to a road. Concealing a site keeps it off a targeting list and forfeits the legal
protection that marking was supposed to buy, and it makes the damage far harder to prove to a
court afterwards. Nobody staffing a regional museum was trained for any of this. The
decisions cannot be deferred until something happens, because the entire point is that they
have to be made first.

---

## What sandbags actually do

The thing worth understanding about sandbagging a monument is what it is for, because it is
not for what most people assume.

A sandbag wall does very little against blast overpressure. The pressure wave from a
near-miss goes over and around it and will still take out an unsupported window, a vault or a
plaster ceiling. What the sand stops is fragmentation: the metal and masonry thrown outward at
high velocity, which is what actually destroys a stone facade, a carved portal or a statue at
street level.

So the standard treatment is a cage rather than a blanket. Scaffolding goes up around the
object, geotextile membrane goes on to keep sand off the stone, and then the sand goes in.
The membrane is not optional and it is the part that gets skipped. Sand holds moisture, and
sand held against limestone for two winters will do damage that no shell came near.

Yuriy Nesterenko, the museum's conservator, has the numbers in his head for every space in
the building.

"Forty-one degrees in the roof space in August," he says. "Four in the cellar in February.
The object wants nineteen and about fifty percent humidity, and I can give it neither, and
those are the good options."

He wears two watches. One is still set to a shift pattern that stopped existing in the first
month.

## Moving it

The collection went out in stages, and the stages were decided by weight and by fragility
rather than by value, which Krylenko says is the thing outsiders find hardest to accept.

Textiles and paper went first, in 11 vans over nine nights, because they are light and
because they tolerate movement better than almost anything else. Ceramics followed. Panel
paintings went last and went slowest, and 19 of them have not gone at all.

"Panel paintings hate two things," Nesterenko says. "Change, and movement. We are offering
both."

A painting on a wooden support is a laminate of materials that respond to humidity at
different rates. Move it from a room at 50 percent relative humidity into a van, then a
cellar at 80, and the wood swells while the paint and ground layers do not, and the result is
cracking, cupping and lifting that no treatment fully reverses. The damage does not appear
during the journey. It appears across the following year, as the support finds its new
equilibrium and the paint layer fails to follow it, which is why an evacuation can be
declared a success in March and be visibly a partial failure by the next winter.

"The van does more damage than the blast would, most days," Nesterenko says. "I want to be
accurate. Most days."

Lidiya Ponomarenko has driven 34 of those runs. She is a volunteer, she is uninsured for any
of it, and she has disconnected the interior light of her van so that opening the door at
night shows nothing.

She was asked, once, to sign for a load without a second signature, and refused, and the
argument that followed took 40 minutes on a road she did not want to be stopped on.

The accountant who required that second signature is Nataliya Bilyk, and she has not relaxed
the rule once.

"Everybody thinks I am being bureaucratic," Bilyk says. "Objects that move without paper
disappear. Sometimes to a thief and sometimes to a filing error, and in twenty years nobody
will be able to tell those apart."

At the receiving end, the collection sits in three locations, one of which is a working mine
at a depth where the temperature holds within about two degrees all year.

"Twenty-six years I asked for a climate-controlled store," Krylenko says. "I got one in eight
days, in a mine, from a man I had never met."

## The lawyer's case

Dr Hanna Zabuzhko thinks Krylenko is wrong, says so directly, and has been saying it for two
years without any expectation of winning.

Zabuzhko is a heritage lawyer. She writes case numbers on the back of her hand during
meetings and transfers them later. Her argument is about what happens in four years, in a
room, in front of a bench.

"Unmarked and unregistered, a site that is struck is a building that fell down," she says.
"That is all it is, evidentially. That is the whole of it."

The 1954 Convention and its 1999 Second Protocol make deliberate attack on cultural property
a crime for which individuals can be held responsible, and there have been convictions. But a
prosecution has to establish that the property had protected status and that the attacker
knew or should have known. Registration, marking and notification are how that is built. An
emblem is not only a request to the enemy. It is a dated, notified, documented assertion
that this building was known to be protected, and that assertion is what a case is built out
of five years later when everyone involved has moved, retired or died.

"I am asking people to do the frightening thing," Zabuzhko says, "because in four years it is
the only thing that will count."

She is careful about how she puts this, and she is not glib about the risk.

"Nobody has to take my advice. I have to keep giving it. If I stop, there is no record at
all."

Krylenko's answer is equally careful and does not concede an inch.

"Hanna is not wrong," she says. "I want that written down. She is not wrong. She is
describing a court, and I am describing Tuesday."

I put each position to the other over three days. Neither moved. Neither expected to, and
neither treated the other as unserious, and I am not going to resolve it here because the
field has not.

## Boilers

Serhiy Kobza, the deputy mayor, would like his objection in the article, and he makes it in
the first two minutes without being asked.

"Say it plainly," he says. "The boiler house serves nine thousand people. The museum serves
an idea."

Fuel, generators and dehumidifiers all come out of the same allocation, and every litre that
runs a dehumidifier in a mine is a litre not heating a building in January. The museum's
generator burns about 14 litres a day when the mains are down, which last winter was 71 days.
The crates cost more than the fuel: 3,140 objects went out in purpose-built cases at a
material cost Bilyk puts at just under nine dollars each, which is the single largest line in
two years of accounts. Kobza is not
posturing and he is not hostile. He signed the fuel authorisation for the museum's generator
in October and expects to sign it again.

"Both things are true," he says. "I will sign it, and I will say this to you, and I would
like the second part printed as well as the first."

Nobody I spoke to at the museum dismissed him. Krylenko's response was that she has no
argument that beats a boiler in winter and has stopped trying to construct one.

"I am not going to win that on merit," she said. "I am going to lose it slowly and keep
working."

## The church at the crossroads

Forty minutes north, at a crossroads I agreed not to describe, there is a church that was hit
in the second winter.

It is late eighteenth century, timber-framed above a stone base, and it was not marked either.
The strike landed in the graveyard about 11 metres from the north wall. The building is still
standing. Almost every window is gone, the north wall is scarred from ground level to about
four metres, and the roof took fragments along its whole length.

Captain Ihor Savchenko's team cleared it over two days before anyone else was allowed in.

"Everybody tells me not to disturb the rubble," he says. "I understand why they say it. I
still have to walk through it."

He works with his gloves off. He knows how that looks and he says there is no other way to
find a wire by feel, and that the wire does not care what century the building is.

What the church demonstrates, standing there with daylight coming through the north side, is
the sandbag lesson in reverse. The wall took fragmentation and survived it, chipped and
pitted and structurally sound. The windows, which were unprotected, took overpressure and
were destroyed at a distance where the wall was barely troubled. Everything Nesterenko says
about what sand does and does not do is legible in one elevation.

The icons were not in it. They had gone eleven months earlier, in Ponomarenko's van, over
Father Dovhan's objection, and he came up to the church three days after the strike and stood
in the nave for a while and then went home.

"I was wrong," he says, and then declines to go any further than that, and changes the
subject to the ledger.

Standing in the graveyard, Savchenko gave me the figure that the whole argument turns on.

His unit has been called to 31 cultural sites in this region. Of those, he says, four had any
form of protective marking, and all four of the marked ones were among the damaged.

He is scrupulous about what that does and does not show.

"Thirty-one is not a study," he says. "Four out of four sounds like a pattern and it is a
very small number. I am telling you what I have seen and I am telling you it is not enough to
conclude from. Everybody wants me to conclude from it."

Zabuzhko, told this, does not dispute the observation and disputes the use.

"Four sites," she says. "I can name you a dozen unmarked ones that were hit. This is how a
rumour becomes a policy, and the policy is the one that leaves us with no evidence."

## Weeks, not afternoons

Roughly 2,000 kilometres away, Tomas Rehak assesses damage from orbit and has never set foot
in the country he maps.

The method is comparison. You need a high-resolution image of the site from before, an image
from after, and enough similarity in season, sun angle and off-nadir angle that the two can be
read against each other. Then you look for roof collapse, debris fields around the structure,
crater signatures, and changes in the shadows a building throws, which is often the first
thing that shows a wall is gone.

What slows it down is not analysis. It is cloud, revisit intervals, and the wait for a
usable second image.

"People want it in an afternoon," Rehak says. "An afternoon gets you a rumour. Verification
takes weeks, and the weeks are the point."

The gap between reported and verified damage is large and permanent. Rehak's own working
list for this region holds 604 reported incidents against 212 he considers verified to a
standard that would survive a hostile lawyer, and he expects roughly half the remainder never
to be resolvable either way, because the imagery does not exist for the right dates.

That is not a scandal. It is the difference between a claim and a confirmed observation when
the observation has to survive challenge.

It also has a price. A commercial tasking of a satellite over a specific site on a specific
day runs into the low thousands of dollars, and the budget Rehak works to bought 47 of them
last year, against a list of 604.

The method's hard limit is the one Rehak states before anybody asks.

"I can tell you a roof is gone," he says. "I cannot tell you what was under it."

That limitation is precisely why Krylenko's exercise book matters, and why Zabuzhko wants it
duplicated, and why Krylenko will not let it out of her bag.

## Footlockers

The precedent everyone in this field cites is Mali.

In 2012 and 2013, during the occupation of the north, an operation moved manuscripts out of
Timbuktu: a very large collection, commonly given as around 350,000 items, carried in
footlockers by road and by river over several months, into Bamako.

Dr Farida Kone was part of it and is careful about how it gets used.

"We told almost no one," she says. "That is why it worked, and I notice that is the part
people leave out when they admire it."

She is more insistent still about the second half.

The manuscripts were saved and then began to suffer. Bamako is wetter than Timbuktu, and the
material had spent centuries stabilised in dry conditions, so a collection that survived an
occupation acquired a mould and humidity problem it had never had in five hundred years. Some
manuscripts were burned anyway, before anyone could move them, and the conservation bill for
the ones that got out is still being paid a decade later.

"You will want to copy us," Kone says. "Copy the footlockers. Do not copy the luck."

Nesterenko has read everything available about the Mali operation and quotes the humidity
outcome at people who bring it up as an unqualified success.

"They did the right thing and it cost them," he says. "That is what the right thing looks
like. It still cost them."

## The east cellar

Anton Marchuk guards the empty building and declined to be interviewed twice before offering,
on the third afternoon, to walk me round at dusk.

He has worked here 14 years. During the site survey that preceded the evacuation, a team
assessed every space in the building for storage suitability and recorded the two cellars as
comparable.

They are not comparable. Marchuk has known this since roughly 2013.

"The east cellar is dry," he says. "It has always been dry. There is a drain under the yard
that runs the wrong way and takes water off it. The west one weeps every spring."

He mentioned this to nobody at the time because nobody asked him, and because, as he put it,
a guard is not consulted about conservation. Nineteen panel paintings that could not travel
are now in the east cellar, on Marchuk's advice, given eventually to Nesterenko in a corridor
in March.

Captain Ihor Savchenko cleared the site before any of them could enter it. He is an engineer
officer, he has a working relationship with the museum that both sides describe as good, and
his priorities are not their priorities.

"Everybody says do not disturb the rubble," he says. "I understand why. I still have to walk
through it." He takes his gloves off to feel for wires, which he knows looks reckless and
which he says is the only way to do it properly.

Father Mykhailo Dovhan wants the icons back and is losing the argument.

Eleven of them came out of his church in the first spring, and he signed for each one in a
parish ledger that has been in use since 1974 and in which his predecessor's handwriting
appears on earlier pages. He accepts the reasoning. He does not accept the conclusion.

"They were painted to be looked at," he says. "In a box, it is only wood."

---

At the end of the walk Marchuk stopped at the top of the yard, where the ground gives you the
whole footprint of the building at once, and pointed out the line of the drain that keeps the
east cellar dry. It runs diagonally under the cobbles and it is not marked on any plan the
museum holds.

Below us, in that cellar, 19 panel paintings sat at about 11 degrees on a February evening,
which is eight degrees below where Nesterenko would like them and roughly 30 above where they
would be in a van.

None of the buildings above them carry a shield. The exercise book was in Krylenko's bag,
where it has been for two years, holding the only complete account of where 3,140 objects
went, in a shorthand four people can read.

I asked her, at the end, what happens to the book.

"It goes back in the bag," she said.
