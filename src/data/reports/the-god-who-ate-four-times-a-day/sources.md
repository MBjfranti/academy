# Evidence spine

- Later Babylonian ritual schedules describe four divine meal services: a great and a little meal in the morning, then both again in the evening.
- Temple personnel paid from the offerings they present, and priestly and craft offices running in families, a pattern attested across Mesopotamian periods.
- The Nippur governor's archive, roughly twelve thousand tablets covering about 1360 to 1220 BC and so contemporary with this article, recording agricultural deliveries and imposts across Babylonia. It is an administrative archive of the governor's household rather than of a temple.
- Marduk's akitu festival attested at Nippur by the thirteenth century, the earliest explicit evidence for it in a southern city other than Babylon, and Marduk and Enlil ideologies in syncretism during the Kassite period.
- YBC 8958 is an Old Babylonian culinary tablet of about two hundred lines with seven detailed bird preparations. It includes serving directions, substitutions, and a note on keeping leftover broth.
- Divine seating, precedence, and portions expressed political and theological hierarchy.


## Chronology note

**Balāṭu lives in Kassite Babylon, about 1226 BC.** He reads the Old Babylonian bird tablet
as an antiquarian object; it predates him by roughly five hundred years, which is the
distance the article claims for it.

An earlier version of this file described a first-millennium setting, and an earlier draft of
the article gave him the Neo-Babylonian prebend: a temple office as heritable property, sold
for silver and divisible into fractions of a month. That system is documented some six
hundred years after his lifetime and has been cut. What remains is hereditary office and
payment in the offerings themselves, which is not period-specific.

The four-service daily timetable is likewise known from much later ritual texts. The article
keeps it, because the site's premise is a working kitchen rather than a survey, but the
standing note now says plainly that the schedule is read backwards onto his century.

## Working interpretation

The dough-sealed pigeon pot is one practical reading of damaged instructions. Balāṭu’s trials, Nabû-ēṭir, and the kitchen incidents are invented.
