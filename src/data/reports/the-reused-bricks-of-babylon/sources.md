# Evidence spine

- Kassite royal inscriptions stamped into bricks, naming the king and the work. Stamped
  bricks of Kurigalzu survive in quantity, and dedicatory brick inscriptions are known for
  other Kassite rulers of the period.
- Foundation deposits placed beneath the corners of major buildings — bricks, metal objects
  and inscribed tablets — addressed to the builder who would one day disturb them.
- Mud-brick manufacture: clay tempered with chaff and dung, trodden, struck into moulds, and
  sun-dried. The seasoning stage is slow, is routinely underestimated in accounts of ancient
  building, and requires extensive flat dry ground plus the labour to turn and stack.
- The competition between brickmaking and agriculture for the same season, the same flat
  land near a settlement, and the same workforce.
- Kassite-period agricultural administration, and the ordinary provisioning of work crews by
  the household commissioning the work.

## Chronology

Balāṭu lives in Kassite Babylon, about 1226 BC. Kurigalzu I and Kurigalzu II and
Nazi-Maruttash are all comfortably in his past, which is why their bricks can be salvage in
his present. See the chronology note in `the-god-who-ate-four-times-a-day/sources.md`.

## Declared reconstruction

**The reuse argument is an inference, not a report.** Mesopotamian brick reuse is entirely
consistent with the economics set out above, and this article reasons from those economics.
It does not rest on a documented case of a specific wall containing specific royal stamps in
disorder, and it should not be read as one.

Arad-Ea, his crew of fourteen, the granary annexe, the collapsed courtyard the bricks came
from, and the miscount that Balāṭu corrects at the end are all invented.

The pot is the site's existing harvest recipe, used here because the argument of the article
is that the building crew and the harvest crew are the same men.
