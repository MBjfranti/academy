# The Purple and the Midden

## What is attested

**The dye and its chemistry.** Shellfish purple comes from the hypobranchial gland of
several rock snails, chiefly *Hexaplex trunculus* (banded dye-murex) and *Bolinus brandaris*
(spiny dye-murex). The two species yield different shades, *trunculus* running toward blue
and violet, *brandaris* redder. The precursor in the gland is colourless. It develops
through yellow, green and blue to purple on exposure to light and air.

This photochemistry is the load-bearing fact of the article and it is not embroidered. The
colour genuinely appears after extraction rather than before, and a person watching it for
the first time is watching a real transformation.

**It is a vat dye.** The dye is substantive and needs no mordant. Cloth leaves the vat in
the reduced, greenish-yellow state and oxidises to purple in air. The "turning on the line"
described in the article follows directly from this, and the detail that a fold pressed
against itself stays green until shaken loose is a property of oxidation, not invention.

**Aegean, and early.** Crushed-shell middens associated with dye working are known from
Bronze Age Aegean coastal sites, on Crete and its offshore islets among others, with
evidence running back well before the industry became associated with the Levantine cities.
Purple working in the Aegean is not a Phoenician import in this period, and the article's
setting on a Messenian shore is a plausible placement rather than a documented works.

**Quantity.** The yield per animal is minute and the commonly cited figures for snails per
gram of pure dye run into the thousands. Anniwiya deliberately never gives a number: Kuwatta
tells her nobody counts snails, and offers a basket-to-a-hem rule instead. That is a
narrative choice made to avoid stating a modern laboratory estimate as a Bronze Age
workshop's own arithmetic.

**Linear B.** Purple-worked textiles are recorded as a distinct category, and a designation
of women workers is built from the same root. Women in the Pylos tablets are frequently
listed as groups by their place of origin on the Anatolian and island coasts rather than by
personal name, with quantities of grain and figs issued against the group. *Knidian women*
and *Milesian women* are both of this type.

## What is inferred

**That the workers ate the snails.** The flesh is edible and shell middens at dye sites
combine industrial waste with food waste, so the two are physically mixed. That the
dye-workers systematically ate what they opened is an argument from the economics: the
animals are already dead, already opened, in the thousands, daily, in front of hungry
people. It is not a documented practice, and the `standing` box says so.

**The smell, the rent, the sheds facing the water.** All consistent with a putrefying
protein industry and none of it attested for this site.

**Crushed shell as floor plaster and track metalling.** Shell is used as aggregate in
several periods and the practice is unremarkable, but no specific instance is being cited
here.

## What is invented

Kuwatta, the seven Knidian women, the works below Pylos, the twelve stirrup jars, and the
particular day. Kuwatta's name is built from plausible Luwian elements rather than taken
from a tablet.

## The connection nobody needs to notice

Kuwatta is recorded as a Knidian. Anniwiya is recorded as a Milesian. Knidos and Millawanda
sit about sixty miles apart on the same Anatolian coast, and both women are at Pylos, both
as groups under a heading, both counted in grain and figs. Anniwiya says the distance out
loud once and then leaves it alone.

## Chronology check

The article is set in 1226 BC. Nothing here depends on the Roman-period accounts of purple
manufacture, which describe lead vats, long boiling and specific steeping regimes. Those are
a thousand years late and are deliberately kept out. What the article describes is limited
to cracking, gland extraction, salting, standing in open vats, and oxidation in air and sun.
