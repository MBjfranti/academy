# Evidence spine

- Nursing to about three years in Egypt, stated in New Kingdom instruction literature and
  broadly consistent with weaning ages recovered from Egyptian cemetery populations by later
  isotope work.
- Supplementary feeding beginning long before nursing ends, so that weaning is a process of
  years rather than an event.
- The rise in child mortality at and after weaning, a pattern general to pre-modern
  populations and driven by the move from milk to water, grain and the household pot.
- Emmer as the dominant Egyptian wheat, eaten as bread and as boiled cracked grain; dairying,
  onions, dates and leafy greens attested in settlement deposits.
- Marsh fowling in the Delta as ordinary subsistence, and kept geese as a household animal.
- Rendered animal fat as a stored good, valued because it keeps when meat does not.
- Nile crocodile and hippopotamus as real and constant hazards of riverside settlement, the
  hippopotamus being the more destructive of the two to crops and to people.
- Egyptians are very rarely shown swimming. Men appear in the water as fishermen in
  difficulty or as drowning enemy soldiers, and a narrative tale turns on a man refusing to
  cross water to reach his brother because of crocodiles. Where swimming instruction is
  attested, as in the Middle Kingdom boast of Kheti about lessons with the king's children,
  it is an elite privilege taught in constructed pools rather than in the river.
- Attested children's games: girls juggling balls thrown between them, painted at Beni Hasan;
  leapfrog; tug of war; wooden spinning tops; balls of leather or woven papyrus stuffed with
  straw; cloth dolls stuffed with reed.

## Declared reconstruction

The village, Iset, her four children and every quantity and gesture in the article are
invented.

**The force-feeding of geese is not claimed for this century.** It is attested in Old
Kingdom tomb scenes at Saqqara and Giza roughly twelve hundred years before this piece is
set, and the purpose of the practice there appears to be fat rather than liver. Whether it
was still done in the New Kingdom Delta is an assumption, so the article rests on marsh
fowling and kept birds instead, and the fat rather than the method carries the argument.

An earlier draft of this article had village children swimming and playing in the open
river. That was wrong and it has been cut. The evidence points the other way: the water is
worked from a bank or a boat, the children work the edge and stay dry, and one adult always
has the job of facing the channel. The correction strengthens the piece rather than
weakening it, because the village's vigilance about the visible danger is what makes the
invisible one land.

The article's central claim — that a weaning child's food is the household pot thinned,
strained and enriched rather than a separate cuisine, and that the fat is what makes it
survivable — is an argument built from the nursing duration, the mortality pattern and the
ingredient corpus. No single Egyptian text states it.
